## What Happened

After finishing third at the Spanish Grand Prix in Madrid, Lando Norris was drinking a Coca-Cola in the cooldown room when an F1 official asked him to put it down, citing the sport's partnership with PepsiCo. Norris refused, on camera: "I just did a race, so I'm allowed to drink what I would like. Thank you so much." The clip spread fast, and most of the reaction sided with him.

Former Haas team principal Guenther Steiner weighed in on The Red Flags Podcast, calling it unprofessional: "If you are a professional, you don't do that. End of story." But Steiner also named exactly what the intervention had actually produced: "If they wouldn't have said anything, nobody would have even noticed, but because it was so bumped up now it's a thing, and now Coca-Cola is laughing."

No penalty followed. The story that outlasted the moment itself wasn't Norris's drink — it was the decision to say something about it.

## Why It Happened

PepsiCo's deal with Formula 1 is real money, not a logo-placement formality. Announced in May 2025 as a multi-year agreement running through 2030, it covers three brands — Sting Energy as Official Energy Drink, Gatorade as Official Sports Drink and F1 Sprint partner, Doritos as Official Savory Snack Partner — and secures TV-visible trackside advertising, Fan Zone activation at 21 races, and broadcast reach into more than 200 territories, according to PepsiCo's own announcement.

The detail that actually explains Madrid: PepsiCo's exclusive track pouring and product supply rights don't kick in until 2026. This is the first season those rights exist. A cooldown room — athlete-facing, camera-adjacent, exactly the kind of space "TV-visible" is written to cover — is precisely where a brand-new exclusivity clause gets tested for the first time. The official wasn't reaching for a rule at random; year one of a new right is when enforcement staff are most primed to act on it, because it's the year the client is watching to see whether the clause actually holds.

## Economic Impact

Sponsorship exclusivity is priced on a counterfactual: what a competitor's brand would have been worth in that exact spot, on camera, if nobody had paid to keep it out. PepsiCo isn't paying for Norris's silence — it's paying so a moment like this never becomes visible in the first place. Steiner's own quote is the honest accounting of what happened instead: nothing was lost by staying quiet, and everything was gained by the rival brand the moment an official turned a two-second sip into a confrontation on camera.

That's the specific failure worth pricing. The clause's entire value proposition is invisibility — a Gatorade cooler where a rival's product used to be, unremarked-on, forever. The instant it gets enforced against a driver who refuses on camera, the exclusivity right itself becomes the content, and the free media it generates flows to whichever brand wasn't the one paying for the rule. In year one of PepsiCo's pouring rights taking effect, the first real test of the clause produced the opposite of what it was bought to produce.

## The Framework

This isn't an F1-specific mistake — it's a known failure mode of any "TV-visible exclusivity" clause across sports sponsorship. The value only shows up when it's invisible: nobody notices the sponsor's product quietly present where a competitor's isn't. The moment enforcement becomes visible against a star athlete who pushes back on camera, the clause itself becomes the story, and the free exposure runs to the excluded brand, not the paying one. Broadcasters who've learned this lesson tend to cut away from logo disputes rather than draw a camera to them; a live, on-mic confrontation in an athlete-facing space does the opposite.

F1 is more exposed to this than most sports because its drivers carry personal brand equity independent of the series itself. Norris's own popularity — not Formula 1's institutional weight — is what turned a two-second clip into a multi-day story. A category sponsor's contract department can write a clause covering every square meter of a cooldown room; it can't write one that survives a popular athlete calmly refusing on camera.

## Verdict

Nothing here cost Norris anything, and nothing here cost PepsiCo money in a strict accounting sense — but the season PepsiCo activated its most exclusive F1 rights yet is the same season its own enforcement handed a direct competitor's brand more organic attention than a paid campaign would have bought in the same two seconds. My read: the clause did exactly what it was written to do, and failed at the only job that actually mattered — staying invisible.
