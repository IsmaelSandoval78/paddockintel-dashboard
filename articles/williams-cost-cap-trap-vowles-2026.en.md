## What happened

At a press conference in Sepang on October 2, 2026, Williams team principal James Vowles put numbers behind a complaint that's been building all season: Formula 1's cost cap, now five years old, has made the competitive gap between the grid's top and bottom worse, not better. "Number of times on the podium used to be 10% of the time, now it's 5% of the time," he said of teams outside the top four. The top four themselves -- Mercedes, Red Bull, McLaren and Ferrari -- now take 83% of all points scored, up from the 70s before the cap existed. Vowles's framing was blunt: the cap had three original jobs -- financial sustainability, sporting equality, and a tighter field -- and "those two have failed."

## Why it happened

The cost cap sets the same hard ceiling for every team regardless of where they're starting from, and that's exactly the mechanism Vowles says traps a team like Williams. A top team's infrastructure -- wind tunnels, simulators, factory systems, headcount -- is already built and paid for from years of higher spending before the cap existed; its entire budget today can go toward car development. A team rebuilding from what Vowles calls "20 years of underinvestment" has to spend a large slice of that same capped budget just reaching the baseline everyone else already has. His own number for that inefficiency: roughly 30% more than it costs a dominant team to get the same basic capability, on "systems that I need to do just to do the very basics," not performance. Williams has "roughly speaking, tripled" its expenditure under his tenure -- money that, by his own account, is still mostly closing a gap rather than building an advantage.

## Economic impact

The clearest number is Williams's own season: a 238-point gap to fourth place, despite the team taking three podiums of its own. That's not a team with no competitive pockets -- it's a team that can produce a great weekend but can't sustain the infrastructure to do it consistently against teams that don't have to spend on catching up. Multiply that gap across the whole non-top-four tier and Vowles's 83/17 points split and 5% podium share follow directly. But two of his peers read the same data differently. Ferrari's Fred Vasseur credits the cap with preventing "five or six teams in bankruptcy or almost in bankruptcy" before 2018 and with attracting new investment into the sport -- the same financial-sustainability goal Vowles agrees was met, framed as the real achievement rather than a consolation prize. Alpine's Flavio Briatore doesn't contest the competitive numbers at all; he blames how the cap is administered, pointing to loopholes between spending categories, and wants everything folded into one budget line rather than several that teams can shuffle between.

## The panorama

This is the same entry-fee economics playing out from the inside. [The anti-dilution fee that priced a new grid seat for BYD or Geely](/byd-geely-f1-entry-fee-anti-dilution-2026) exists precisely because F1's current commercial value is concentrated at the top -- a new entrant pays for access to that concentration, not to an equal split of it. Vowles is describing the same concentration from the other direction: a fixed spending ceiling that locks in whoever already held the infrastructure advantage when the cap started. It's also why the facilities arms race matters as much as the cap itself -- [Audi's new Hinwil campus](/audi-hinwil-campus-f1-campus-ag-2026), under construction well before its October announcement, is exactly the kind of capital-intensive infrastructure spend Vowles says smaller teams can't make without cutting into car development. The cap controls how much anyone can spend each year; it was never built to equalize what each team was already carrying in when it began.

## Verdict

Vowles isn't accusing F1 of a broken rule -- he's describing a rule that did exactly what a hard spending ceiling with no mechanism for closing starting-position gaps was always going to do: freeze relative advantage in place once imposed, rather than erode it over time. The financial-sustainability goal worked because it was the one goal a flat cap is naturally suited to deliver. Sporting equality and field compression needed something else entirely -- a floor-closing mechanism, not just a ceiling -- and neither Vasseur's nor Briatore's response actually disputes that gap; they're arguing about whether it's worth the trade, and how to patch the administration around it. The 83-17 split is the real number from this one. What happens to it is a decision F1's governance has to make deliberately, not a bug anyone can patch mid-season.
