## What Happened

In November 2025, Toto Wolff sold 15% of his own holding company's stake in Mercedes F1 to George Kurtz, founder and CEO of the cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike. The sale amounted to 5% of the team overall, cost Kurtz $300 million, and set Mercedes' valuation at roughly $6 billion — the highest of any Formula 1 team. Kurtz joins Mercedes-Benz, INEOS, and Wolff himself on the team's ownership register, and takes a seat on its strategic steering committee alongside Wolff, Mercedes-Benz CEO Ola Kallenius, and INEOS's Sir Jim Ratcliffe.

Through round 16 of the 2026 season, Mercedes leads the constructors' championship with 556 points and 11 race wins — more than double the points of third-placed McLaren. The valuation and the results arrived in the same window, not in spite of each other.

## Why It Happened

Kurtz wasn't a stranger writing a cold check. CrowdStrike was already a commercial partner of the Mercedes F1 team before this deal, which makes the purchase closer to a sponsor converting into an owner than an outside investor spotting an undervalued asset. That matters for how the price got set: Kurtz bought in knowing the team's on-track trajectory and its commercial position from the inside, not from public reporting alone.

The ownership structure itself has stayed remarkably stable while its value has not. Mercedes-Benz and INEOS have each held exactly a third of the team since INEOS bought in for £208 million in December 2020 — a deal that valued the team at roughly £624 million at the time. Wolff's slice has simply gotten smaller in percentage terms (down from an even third to 28.33%) while growing enormously in dollar terms, because the pie it's a fraction of has grown by a factor of more than seven in five years.

## Economic Impact

$6 billion for 5% works out to a round-number valuation that's easy to state and easy to compare — which is exactly why it's worth comparing. [PaddockIntel already covered Aston Martin's ownership structure](/aston-martin-alonso-stroll-2027-ownership-2026), a team valued at $3.2 billion in a funding round the same general period, while finishing 2026 10th of 11 constructors with 7 points. Mercedes, at nearly double that valuation, finished the same stretch of races 1st, with 556. Two F1 teams, two very different investment theses: one priced on the bet that a new facility, a new designer, and a new engine partner eventually turn results around; the other priced on a team that is already winning and already profitable, where the money is buying a stake in a going concern rather than a recovery story.

That distinction matters for how to read F1 team valuations generally. A number like "$6 billion" or "$3.2 billion" says nothing on its own about whether an investor is paying for proven performance or for the right to wait. Mercedes' case is the cleaner one: the valuation and the championship table point the same direction, which is unusual enough in major team-sport ownership to be the actual story, not a footnote to it.

## The Framework

[PaddockIntel's look at Mercedes' cost-cap strategy around its Microsoft partnership](/microsoft-mercedes-f1-cost-cap) covered a different side of the same team: how Mercedes manages what counts against its spending limit. This piece is about who owns the result of that spending, and the pattern worth naming is that F1 team ownership has split into two distinct investor profiles. One is the turnaround bet — see Aston Martin, or the broader wave of outside capital (BYD and Geely among the names PaddockIntel has tracked) chasing teams that aren't yet competitive but have a credible path to becoming so. The other is the Mercedes profile: capital arriving specifically because the team is already delivering, priced at a premium for certainty rather than a discount for risk.

Both are rational bets. They're just not the same bet, and treating every nine-figure or ten-figure F1 valuation as evidence of the same underlying story — "team sport ownership is booming" — misses which kind of booming each number actually represents.

## Verdict

Mercedes didn't need outside capital to prove it could win; it's been winning. What the Kurtz deal actually demonstrates is that F1's most successful team is also now its most expensive to buy a piece of, and that the two facts are connected rather than coincidental. My read: watch what happens to Aston Martin's valuation once — if — its own wind tunnel correlation problems resolve and the results catch up to the infrastructure spend. The real test of whether F1 ownership stakes are pricing performance or pricing potential will be whether that valuation moves before the points column does, or after.
