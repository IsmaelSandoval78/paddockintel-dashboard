## What Happened

Christian Horner was removed from operational duties at Red Bull Racing on July 9, 2025, after 20 years in charge. The exit wasn't finalized until September 22, 2025, when Red Bull and Horner agreed a settlement that press estimates at the time put somewhere between £52 million and $107 million, depending on which outlet's sourcing you trusted. A year later, the real number surfaced through an unglamorous route: Red Bull Racing Ltd and Red Bull Technology Ltd's own UK Companies House accounts, filed and reported on October 7, 2026, recording £100.48 million in combined "compensation for loss of office" to each company's highest-paid director. Neither filing names Horner, and neither he nor Red Bull has put a number on the record — but he was the highest-paid director of both entities in the year he left, which is how outlets have closed the gap between an anonymous accounting line and a named executive. His contract had been due to run until 2030. The settlement is understood to include a gardening leave period keeping him out of a rival garage for months afterward, which is the direct reason Horner is only now surfacing as a real candidate for other teams — including, per this week's reporting, Ferrari, where speculation about him replacing Fred Vasseur is exactly why this is worth totaling up now.

Horner's case is the largest and most public, but it isn't unique. Ferrari removed Mattia Binotto after the 2022 season despite Ferrari finishing second in the constructors' championship that year — reportedly paying him roughly $3 million, his full salary for 12 months, to sit out rather than join a competitor immediately. Alpine went further in the other direction: Otmar Szafnauer was dismissed as team principal in July 2023, 18 months into the job, informed by a video call from Renault's head of HR — with no severance figure ever disclosed publicly.

## Why It Happened

Three different exits, three very different price tags, and the reason isn't performance alone — it's contract structure. Senior F1 team principal contracts, especially at UK-based teams, typically bundle two protections that make an early termination expensive and slow rather than quick and cheap: payment of the contract's remaining value (absent conduct serious enough to void that protection), and a gardening-leave or non-compete clause that keeps the departing executive off a rival's payroll for a defined period.

Horner's payout scales with what was left on the table — a contract running five more years to 2030 makes for a much bigger number than Binotto's, whose deal had only a year left when Ferrari moved him out. Szafnauer's case looks different again: no confirmed severance figure has surfaced, and the manner of his dismissal (a same-day video call, mid-season) reads like a team unwilling or unable to negotiate a long, paid exit — a smaller team's budget constraints showing up in how a departure gets handled, not just in what it costs.

## Economic Impact

Lined up together, the three cases span roughly two orders of magnitude: Horner's filing-confirmed £100.48 million dwarfs Binotto's reported $3 million, and both dwarf Szafnauer's undisclosed figure, which by every account was resolved without the kind of prolonged gardening-leave payout the other two received. That spread is itself informative — it tracks contract length remaining and negotiating leverage, not results on track, since Binotto's Ferrari had just finished second in the championship and Horner's Red Bull had just finished third the year of his removal, a worse position than Ferrari's.

None of this includes what a team spends replacing the departed principal, absorbing a leadership transition mid-development-cycle, or managing the technical staff turnover that sometimes follows a change at the top — real costs, but ones without a publicly disclosed figure attached to any of these three cases, so this article doesn't put a number on them.

## The Framework

The number a team pays to remove its principal only means something next to what it bought. Checked directly against each team's own constructors' championship record:

**Red Bull:** dominant under Horner in 2022 (P1, 759 points) and 2023 (P1, 860 points), the team's position had already slipped to P3 in both 2024 and 2025 — the season of his July removal — before falling further to P4 through the first 15 rounds of 2026. The decline started well before Horner left and has continued since.

**Ferrari:** Binotto's final full season (2022) produced P2 and 554 points. Vasseur's first year in charge (2023) was worse by both measures — P3, 406 points — before climbing to Ferrari's best season under either principal in 2024 (P2, 652 points), then falling to P4 in 2025, and sitting P2 again through Baku in 2026. [PaddockIntel's breakdown of what F1 prize money actually pays out by position](/melbourne-2026-f1-prize-money-constructors-championship) shows exactly how much each of those swings is worth at season's end — and Ferrari's four years under Vasseur have now touched three different tiers of that payout table.

**Alpine:** already declining before Szafnauer arrived (P4 in 2022 to P6 in his one full season, 2023), the team got worse for two more years after his removal — P6 in 2024, then a worst-of-the-run P10 in 2025 — before recovering to P6 in 2026. Removing him didn't arrest the slide; if anything, the slide continued for two more seasons.

Three teams, three leadership changes, and not one produced a better championship position the very next season. That doesn't mean the moves were wrong — Ferrari's 2024 rebound suggests Vasseur's project eventually worked — but it does mean the invoice and the payoff arrive on different timelines, if the payoff arrives at all.

## Verdict

The Horner-to-Ferrari speculation dominating this week's paddock conversation isn't happening because Ferrari is in crisis — [the team sits second in the 2026 constructors' championship](/f1-2026-qatar-abu-dhabi-hosting-fee-standoff), comfortably clear of McLaren and Red Bull. It's happening because Horner's own gardening leave clock is finally running out, freeing up the sport's highest-profile available executive at the moment his old employer is still paying for having let him go. The larger pattern holds regardless of how this particular rumor resolves: firing an F1 team principal is one of the most expensive decisions a team can make, priced by how much contract is left rather than how the team is performing, and none of the three most-discussed cases from the last four seasons produced a better result the following year. The bill is real and it's due almost immediately. The turnaround, if it comes, is neither guaranteed nor quick.
