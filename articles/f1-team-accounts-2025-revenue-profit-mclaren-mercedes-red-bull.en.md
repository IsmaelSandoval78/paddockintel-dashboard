## What happened

The British-based Formula 1 teams have filed their 2025 accounts at Companies House, the UK company registry, between June and October 2026. PaddockIntel read the eight filings for the teams' main UK companies and checked every figure below against the scanned statements. Mercedes-Benz Grand Prix was the largest and most profitable: revenue of £633.4 million and a profit after tax of £125.9 million. McLaren Racing, which won the 2025 constructors' championship with 833 points in PaddockIntel's database, lost £17.4 million.

Every full team grew its revenue. McLaren's rose 20% from £488.4 million, Williams' 36% from £179.8 million, Aston Martin's 23% and Alpine's 21%, while Mercedes was flat on a restated £632.1 million. Three of the six full teams lost money.

## Why it happened

McLaren's loss came from its ownership change, not from the racing. Its strategic report says revenue growth was offset by higher costs "largely due to payments of share based payments". The charge rose to £136.2 million from £38.2 million, taking employee costs to £285.6 million. In September 2025 the team repaid the loan notes held by investors led by MSP Sports Capital since 2020, valued at £179 million at the end of 2024, and McLaren Group, majority-owned by Bahrain's Mumtalakat with CYVN Holdings as a minority shareholder, took full ownership. The highest-paid director received £8.2 million in pay plus £73.7 million in long-term incentive payments, the accounts show; they do not name the director.

The losses elsewhere have different causes. Aston Martin's AMR GP paid £32.7 million in interest, more than its £20.4 million operating loss. Williams roughly halved its loss to £25.1 million as revenue grew faster than costs. Alpine Racing, the Enstone chassis company, returned to profit after a £14.6 million loss in 2024, despite finishing last in the 2025 constructors' championship. The two US-owned teams file only for their UK arms, which work for their American parents: Haas Formula UK provides services to Haas Formula LLC, and Cadillac's UK company spent its pre-debut year building the team, with almost no revenue.

## Economic impact

Revenue in these accounts includes prize money, sponsorship and commercial income; Red Bull Technology's filing lists sponsorship, prize money, bonus payments, hospitality and contributions towards the race programme. By PaddockIntel's calculation, the six full UK teams brought in £2.62 billion between them in 2025 and made a combined £68.6 million after tax, a margin of about 2.6%. Without Mercedes, the other five lost £57.3 million together.

The prize fund behind part of that revenue is paid mostly on the previous year's constructors' position, Liberty Media's filings say, as we set out in [our analysis of F1's 2026 team payments](/f1-team-payments-2026-prize-money-liberty-media-q3). McLaren won the title in 2024 as well as 2025, so its 2025 revenue already reflected a champion's share; its loss is the clearest sign that the result on track and the result in the accounts can diverge. The cost cap limits most of what a team can spend on the car, as explained in [how the cap reshaped the midfield](/f1-cost-cap-midfield-podiums-vowles-data-2026), but not one-off ownership costs such as McLaren's share-based payments.

Size is no guarantee either way. Williams, with 1,151 staff, employs more people than Red Bull's group, and Aston Martin's AMR GP almost as many, but neither turned a profit. Mercedes made £166.7 million at the operating level with 1,348 staff, while the other five full teams posted a combined operating loss of £42.1 million.

## The framework

These figures are not a complete league table of F1 finances. Each company's scope differs: Mercedes and Williams report groups, McLaren and Aston Martin single companies, and Red Bull's group covers Red Bull Racing Ltd but not Red Bull Powertrains, which files separately. For the manufacturers that build their own engines, that work sits in separate companies, while customer teams pay for their engines within these costs. Ferrari and Racing Bulls in Italy and Audi in Switzerland do not file team accounts in the UK, and Haas and Cadillac file only for subsidiaries.

What the accounts do show is the shape of the business. Revenue across the six full teams ranges from about £244 million to £633 million, headcounts cluster between roughly 1,000 and 1,350, and profit depends less on revenue than on ownership costs, debt and investment. Mercedes' margin of about 20% after tax is the exception; break-even or a loss is closer to the norm.

## Verdict

The filings say F1 teams are growing businesses with thin and uneven profits. Mercedes made £125.9 million after tax while the other five full UK teams lost £57.3 million between them, and the world champion was one of them.

My read: McLaren's loss is an ownership event, not a warning sign. The share-based payments and the repaid investor notes close the chapter that began with outside funding in 2020, and the team enters 2026 wholly owned by McLaren Group. The more telling numbers are Aston Martin's interest bill and Williams' shrinking but persistent loss: they show how much it costs to buy competitiveness under a cost cap, and how long it takes to pay off.
