## What happened

Formula 1's owner no longer has a controlling shareholder. On November 4, Liberty Media acquired all 2,316,537 of the high-vote Series B shares held by John Malone and related entities, about 97% of that class, in exchange for 602,302 low-vote Series A shares and 5,420,699 non-voting Series C shares, its filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission show. Malone's voting power fell from about 48% to about 3%, and no holder now has more than 9% of Liberty's votes, the company said on November 5.

Malone stepped down as chairman emeritus and stays on as a senior advisor. Liberty put the premium in the exchange ratio, 2.6 shares of common stock for each Series B share, at about 1.5% of its equity market value as of October 30. The deal was negotiated and approved by a special committee of independent directors chaired by Liberty director Chase Carey. The same day, the board adopted a one-year rights plan, the defence known as a poison pill.

## Why it happened

The trigger was outside interest. Malone had received "a number of unsolicited offers from third parties" for his Series B shares, Liberty's announcement says, and the company chose to buy them itself. Each Series B share carries ten votes, against one for Series A and none for Series C, Liberty's annual report explains. By PaddockIntel's calculation from the share counts in Liberty's latest quarterly report, Malone's 2.3 million Series B shares were about 0.9% of its 250.8 million shares outstanding, but whoever held them held close to half the votes in the company that owns F1.

Liberty's stated aim was to keep that position from changing hands privately. The deal avoids "the potential transfer of a significant voting position in Liberty Media to a third party in a transaction in which other Liberty Media shareholders would not participate," Carey said. Malone said it lets him increase his economic ownership "while distributing voting power more broadly among Liberty Media's other shareholders." The filings do not name the parties that made offers.

## Economic impact

Control of Liberty is, in practice, control of F1. By PaddockIntel's calculation from the filings, Formula 1 produced about 87% of Liberty's revenue in the first nine months of 2026, $2,496 million of $2,871 million, and about 88% of its adjusted OIBDA, $649 million of $740 million. In 2025 the shares were 86% and 89%. MotoGP and minority investments make up most of the rest.

Until November 4, close to half of those votes could have changed hands in one private transaction, with Malone's stake. Liberty's own annual report warned that this concentration "could discourage others from initiating any potential merger, takeover or other change of control transaction." Now it would take the board's agreement or a deal open to all shareholders. The rights plan reinforces that for a year: anyone who acquires 10% or more of the voting power without board approval, 15% for a passive institutional investor, faces substantial dilution, until November 3, 2027.

Liberty's February annual report also listed a board elected in staggered three-year terms and a requirement of two-thirds of the voting power, or 75% of the board, to approve a merger or the sale of substantially all its assets; the company has since moved its incorporation from Delaware to Nevada, in May, and the filings we read do not say whether those terms changed. For teams, promoters and broadcasters nothing changes directly: the 2026 Concorde Agreement that sets the teams' prize fund, analysed in [our breakdown of F1's 2026 team payments](/f1-team-payments-2026-prize-money-liberty-media-q3), and F1's management are untouched.

## The framework

Dual-class shares are common in US media companies, and they usually exist to keep a founder or a family in charge. Malone's Series B, less than 1% of Liberty's shares, carried about half of its votes. Unwinding that structure is the opposite move: voting power now roughly follows economic ownership, which Liberty says could also "broaden the institutional investor base" in its shares.

The cost to other shareholders was a premium of about 1.5% of Liberty's market value, paid in new shares. The benefit, as the board frames it, is that a future sale of control has to be negotiated with everyone. Outside money has been chasing F1 from other angles too: Apple took over its US broadcast rights from 2026 in a deal reported at about $140 million a year, as we noted in [how Disney responded to losing them](/verstappen-max-vs-100-disney-f1-rights-economics).

## Verdict

The filings say the most valuable stake in F1 just disappeared. Close to half the votes in its owner, a stake that could have been sold in a private deal, have been turned into ordinary shares, and no one holds more than 9%.

My read: the unsolicited offers are the real news. Third parties were willing to pay for a near-half voting stake in F1's owner, and Liberty closed that door. With the poison pill expiring in November 2027, the next approach for F1 will have to come through the front door: an offer to Liberty's board and every shareholder, at a price that reflects about 87% of the company's revenue.
