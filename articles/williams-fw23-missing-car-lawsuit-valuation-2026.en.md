## What Happened

Williams has filed a High Court claim in London to recover a Formula 1 car it says has gone missing for more than a decade. The car is an FW23, the BMW V10-powered chassis Williams raced in 2001 with Ralf Schumacher and Juan Pablo Montoya — one of only nine FW23s built. Williams loaned it in 2015 to Autotech Performance, a garage in Telford, England, as a display piece under a formal loan agreement.

Williams asked for the car back in 2021, writing to Autotech that October to arrange an inspection ahead of its return. It got no reply. Autotech went into liquidation and was struck off the companies register in November 2023. Williams now alleges that Peter Thorpe, Autotech's sole director and shareholder, removed the car from the garage and told the liquidators it was his own property, not Williams'.

The claim, reported this week by PlanetF1, RacingNews365 and Grandprix.com, seeks the car's return plus hire charges — or, failing that, damages of more than £1 million. Williams' own filing puts the car's value at £750,000. Thorpe's lawyers dispute even that: they describe it as a show car with no engine, say he closed Autotech's operations in June 2021, and say memory problems from a stroke in October 2018 mean he isn't sure what happened to the car or who owned it. Where it is now is not established in any of the reporting.

## Why It Happened

This is a contract and custody failure, not a mystery about the car's existence. Williams had a signed loan agreement with Autotech — barrister Richard Clegg has told the court the car was part of the team's "unique collection of its own heritage cars" — but a loan agreement is not the same as an ongoing inspection regime. Six years passed between the 2015 loan and Williams' first recorded attempt to check on the car. That gap gave a struggling garage time to fail, liquidate, and — on Williams' telling — let its director walk off with a Formula 1 chassis before anyone representing the car's actual owner was back in the building.

Thorpe's defense is built entirely around the liquidation period: he says he last visited the garage on 8 August 2021 for the liquidators' own asset valuation and isn't certain the FW23 was still there by December that year. If that holds up, the car didn't vanish from Williams' custody — it vanished during someone else's bankruptcy process, in the gap between a struck-off company's last records and whatever happened to its physical assets afterward. Nobody disputes that Williams delivered the car to Autotech in 2015. What's contested is everything after that: who had it, who removed it, and whether anyone currently knows.

## Economic Impact

Set aside who's liable and look only at the numbers Williams itself put in the claim. The team is suing for the car's return or £1 million-plus in damages, built on its own £750,000 valuation. That number is defensible for a non-running display chassis with no documented recent competition history — exactly what Thorpe's side argues this car is. But it's not what a comparable, race-ready 2001-era Formula 1 car is actually worth on the open market right now.

Michael Schumacher's title-winning Ferrari F2001 — built, raced, and retired the same season as the missing FW23 — sold for €15.98 million at RM Sotheby's Monaco auction in 2025, the most expensive Ferrari F1 car ever sold at public auction. Williams' own marque record is lower but still dwarfs the FW23's claimed value: Nigel Mansell's 1991 title-winning FW14 sold for €4.055 million at the same auction house in 2022, and his 1992 FW14B went for £2.703 million at Bonhams Goodwood in 2019. None of these are the missing car, and none of them are strictly comparable — a title-winning chassis with a famous driver attached commands a premium a mid-grid also-ran car never will. But they establish the real ceiling of this asset class, and it's 5 to 20 times higher than the number currently sitting in Williams' court filing.

That gap matters beyond this one case. A Formula 1 chassis is now a seven-figure-and-up collector asset, priced by a maturing auction market that didn't exist in anything like its current form when most teams' heritage-car loan practices were set. Williams valuing its own missing car at a fraction of what the broader market has shown contemporaries are worth isn't dishonesty — a non-running display car genuinely is worth less than a race-ready title winner — but it is a signal that the team's own custody and valuation practices for its heritage fleet haven't caught up to what that fleet is now worth to collectors.

## The Framework

No other F1 team has litigated a lost or disputed loaned heritage chassis before — this appears to be the first case of its kind in F1 reporting. That's notable given how much value now sits in team heritage fleets. The pattern elsewhere in the sport is to either keep historic cars in-house, in dedicated heritage facilities with component-level logging, or sell complete cars outright to collectors at auction, where title and condition get scrutinized by professionals with money on the line. Loaning a car out to a third party for display, with no evidence of a recurring inspection clause, sits awkwardly between those two models: Williams kept legal ownership but gave up the kind of continuous oversight that protects an asset this valuable.

The broader industry lesson is about asset management catching up to asset value. Classic car and memorabilia markets have repriced Formula 1 history upward for a decade — the Mercedes-Benz W196R's $53.9 million sale at RM Sotheby's Stuttgart in 2025 is the clearest sign of where this market's ceiling now sits. Teams built their heritage-car practices before that repricing happened. This case is what it looks like when a team's paperwork hasn't kept pace with its own history's market value.

## Verdict

Whatever the High Court decides about Peter Thorpe's liability, the more interesting number in this case isn't the one in dispute — it's the one nobody's disputing. Williams is suing for £750,000 to £1 million over a car from the same season a contemporary sold for nearly €16 million. That's not a valuation error so much as evidence that F1 teams have been slow to treat their own heritage fleets as the appreciating, closely-trackable assets the auction market has already decided they are. The lesson here isn't really about one missing chassis. It's that F1 history is worth more than the paperwork protecting it currently assumes.
