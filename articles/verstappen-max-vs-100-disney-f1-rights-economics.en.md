## What Happened

On September 16, four-time champion Max Verstappen started at the back of a 101-driver kart grid at Silverstone and raced to the front in 14 laps, passing 64 karts on the opening lap alone. His field: Red Bull athletes from other sports, streamers, content creators, and members of the public. The event, "Max vs 100," streamed live and free to subscribers on Disney+ and the ESPN app, produced by Whisper with live race data and game-inspired graphics built for a broadcast audience, not a competition jury.

Verstappen's own explanation was nostalgia, not competition: a chance to "go back to basics" and revisit the karting years he raced from age four to fifteen, when his father Jos drove him across Europe to race weekends. Disney's own announcement called it "first-of-its-kind" — language built for a press release, not a results sheet.

Nothing about the event paid points, prize money at championship scale, or moved a standings table anywhere.

## Why It Happened

The event's placement is the tell. Eleven months earlier, in October 2025, Apple signed a five-year deal worth a reported $700 million to take over exclusive U.S. broadcast rights to every Formula 1 race starting in 2026 — about $140 million a year, replacing ESPN, which had held the rights for a reported $85 million a year on average. ESPN's run as F1's American broadcaster ended with that deal.

"Max vs 100" aired less than a year later, produced jointly by Disney+, ESPN, and Red Bull — the same broadcaster that no longer owns a single Grand Prix, running a live global event built entirely around F1's biggest active star. That's not a coincidence of timing; it's the only kind of F1 content a broadcaster without league rights can still buy: a bespoke deal with one team and one driver, negotiated directly, outside of Formula 1's own media rights structure entirely.

## Economic Impact

Verstappen's own pay is the control variable here, and it doesn't move. His Red Bull base salary was reported at $70 million for 2026, and a new contract signed in August 2026 — reported at roughly €92 million a year in base salary from 2027, running through 2030 — was negotiated independent of anything that happens on a go-kart track. A one-off exhibition, however large the audience, doesn't touch a number set by a multi-year championship contract.

What it does move is Red Bull's and Disney's side of the ledger. For Red Bull, it's a marketing asset already on the payroll, activated on a weekend with no Grand Prix to sell — the karting stunt costs a production budget, not an appearance fee at anything close to Verstappen's actual market rate. For Disney/ESPN, it's the cheaper of two ways to stay inside the F1 conversation: the other was $140 million a year for the actual races, which Apple now owns. A single-driver content deal, even a large one, is a rounding error next to that number.

The trade only works because it isn't priced like broadcast rights at all. Nobody pays Formula 1 for the right to put Verstappen in a go-kart — Red Bull owns that access directly through his employment contract, and Disney's side of the deal is a production and distribution agreement, not a league rights fee. That's the entire mechanism: personal-brand content doesn't require owning the league.

## The Framework

This is the same fracture playing out across sports media generally: a league's broadcast rights and an athlete's personal-brand content rights are now two separate markets, sold to two different buyers, on two different timelines. A network that loses the first can still buy the second — an NBA team's local broadcast rights and a star player's own YouTube or Twitch deal already operate this way, priced and negotiated independently of each other.

F1 is unusually exposed to this because its economics concentrate personal brand value in a handful of drivers rather than distributing it across a large roster, the way team sports do. Verstappen, Hamilton, and a few others carry commercial weight that exists mostly independent of which network airs the races. Losing the league deal doesn't cut a broadcaster off from F1 content entirely — it just forces them to negotiate driver by driver, team by team, for whatever access money can still buy directly.

## Verdict

Apple bought the races. Disney bought the driver. Neither of those numbers is really about karting — one is a five-year, $700 million broadcast contract, the other is a production budget attached to a personal-brand deal Red Bull already controlled. My read: losing exclusive league rights doesn't end a broadcaster's relationship with a sport anymore, it just repriced it — from a league-wide rights fee to whatever it costs to rent one driver for an afternoon.
