## What Happened

Mohammed Ben Sulayem confirmed direct talks with both BYD and Geely over a possible 12th F1 entry, but drew a firm line: any newcomer joins on F1's existing terms, not its own. The comment followed BYD reportedly declining to simply buy an existing team outright — the cheaper, faster route several past entrants have taken instead of building a new grid slot from scratch.

Ben Sulayem pointed both manufacturers toward F1's 2031 rules cycle as the logical entry window. Neither has filed a formal bid.

## Why It Happened

F1's terms have a specific, escalating price tag: the anti-dilution fee, paid by any new entrant to compensate the existing teams for splitting the prize pool one or two ways further. It isn't fixed. The 2021-2025 Concorde Agreement set it at a flat $200 million. General Motors and TWG Global actually paid $450 million to bring Cadillac onto the 2026 grid — more than double the treaty rate, though it remains unclear whether that reflects a new standard price or a fee GM specifically negotiated. Going into the current Concorde cycle, existing teams pushed to raise the figure further still, with reported targets as high as $600 million.

The 2031 timing isn't arbitrary either. F1's current power-unit regulations run through the end of 2030. A manufacturer joining under the existing 2026-2030 engine rules would be adopting a formula it had no part in designing, running it for a handful of seasons, then switching again when the next cycle starts. Entering at the 2031 reset instead means joining a rules conversation from its first vote, not its last few years.

## Economic Impact

Every dollar of the anti-dilution fee is a direct transfer to the ten or eleven teams already on the grid — Cadillac's $450 million became real, disclosed cash in the pockets of its competitors, not F1's own balance sheet. That's the mechanism turning "should F1 add a 12th team" into a question every current team has a financial stake in answering "yes, at the right price": a bigger fee is a bigger one-time payout, on top of a smaller ongoing prize-money share once the pool splits one way further.

It also sets a real floor under what a grid slot is worth. If a brand-new entrant has to pay $450 million just to exist on the grid before building a single car, that number becomes the reference point for what an existing team's slot — with a factory, personnel, and a technical track record already built — should be worth in a sale. [PaddockIntel found the same dynamic in Alpine's ownership fight](/alpine-f1-ownership-otro-capital-renault-veto-2026): a headline valuation claim is worth less than a number an actual counterparty has paid or rejected, and Cadillac's $450 million is one of the few genuinely disclosed numbers in the entire conversation.

BYD and Geely have their own reason to pay it anyway. F1's own sponsorship market shows what a grid presence is worth to a brand trying to reach a global, high-income audience — the same logic that's pulled Oracle, HP, Mastercard, and a wave of AI vendors onto cars and pit walls in the last two years. For a Chinese manufacturer trying to establish itself outside its home market, F1 exposure is a marketing budget line, not just a racing one.

## The Framework

The anti-dilution fee is F1's version of an expansion-franchise fee in US sports — a price the league sets not to fund operations, but to compensate the incumbents for the value a new competitor takes from a fixed prize pool and a fixed number of constructor championship points. The difference is that F1's fee isn't published or fixed the way an NFL or NBA expansion fee typically is. It's negotiated team by team, entrant by entrant, which is exactly why Cadillac's real $450 million and the reported $600 million ask for the next team tell two different stories about the same number.

Pointing BYD and Geely toward 2031 does double duty for F1: it defers a concrete decision years past this news cycle, and it lets the anti-dilution fee keep climbing in the meantime, since every year F1's media rights and sponsorship revenue grow is another year the "compensation" incumbents demand for dilution grows with it.

## Verdict

BYD or Geely joining F1 was never really a question of interest — both have it. It's a question of price, and the price is moving in exactly one direction. My read: neither manufacturer signs anything before 2031, and when one finally does, the anti-dilution fee it pays will make Cadillac's $450 million look like the discount rate.
