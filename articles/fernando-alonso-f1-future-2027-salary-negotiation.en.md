## What Happened

Fernando Alonso told reporters ahead of this weekend's Spanish Grand Prix in Madrid that an announcement on his 2027 future is "not soon." Asked why he was drawing out the decision, he replied: "Because I think it's nice, no?" In the same round of comments, published September 11, Alonso confirmed for the first time that a World Endurance Championship program with Aston Martin — not just a Formula 1 seat — is a real option for next year. Asked directly, he answered: "Yes."

His current Aston Martin contract runs out at the end of 2026. The team has reportedly countered his ask for 2027-28 with a Letter of Intent covering a raise from his current €30 million salary to around €40 million — a deal that would make him the grid's third-highest earner, level with Charles Leclerc and behind only Max Verstappen and Lewis Hamilton.

## Why It Happened

Alonso has been explicit that his decision hinges on the 2027 rules, not on Aston Martin's current form. He's called this generation of cars unpleasant to drive, specifically criticizing how they reward drivers for charging batteries in high-speed corners rather than attacking them. He thinks 2027's regulations are "a step in the right direction" but has said, in his own words, he doesn't yet know "if it's enough."

That's a genuinely open technical question, not posturing — the FIA has confirmed no further changes are planned before the 2027 rules land, so Alonso is betting on a car he hasn't driven yet. Adrian Newey, brought in as Aston Martin's Managing Technical Partner specifically to fix the issues Alonso has criticized, has been the one publicly reassuring the paddock: "I'm pretty confident that Fernando is enjoying his time with us and that we will continue with our relationship." Notably, that's Newey's line, not Alonso's.

## Economic Impact

The number that matters here isn't whether Alonso stays. It's what staying costs, and what not staying protects. A jump from €30 million to a reported €40 million a year is a 33% raise for a 45-year-old driver whose team spent most of 2026 outside the podium picture — a figure Aston Martin is reportedly willing to pay specifically because Alonso's leverage isn't confined to his own cockpit, a dynamic [Aston Martin's driver-salary structure already reflects across the grid](/f1-nascar-indycar-driver-salaries-2026/).

By keeping the WEC option explicitly live, Alonso turns what looks like a two-way choice — stay or retire — into a three-way negotiation: Formula 1 driver, some other role "helping the team," or a seat in Aston Martin's own Valkyrie hypercar program, entering its third World Endurance Championship and IMSA season in 2027. Every one of those paths keeps him inside the Aston Martin ecosystem, which is exactly why Newey sounds confident and exactly why Alonso has no reason to rush an answer: every option on the table still ends with a paycheck from Lawrence Stroll's operation, an operation already [committing nine figures to fix what Alonso is publicly criticizing](/adrian-newey-aston-martin-2026-investment-crisis/).

The closed door matters as much as the open ones. Alpine locked in Pierre Gasly and Franco Colapinto for 2027 in late August, eliminating the one alternative — a reunion with Flavio Briatore — that could have given Alonso real outside leverage against Aston Martin specifically. With that door shut, his negotiating position now runs entirely through what Aston Martin is willing to structure inside its own walls, not through what a rival team would pay to poach him.

## The Framework

This is a familiar shape in sports business, just unusually explicit about it: a franchise player nearing the end of a playing career gets offered a hybrid deal — a reduced on-track role paired with expanded ambassador or technical value — instead of a clean release, because the commercial and technical value of the name outlasts peak performance. What's unusual in Formula 1 specifically is that Aston Martin has a second live racing program that can actually absorb that value with an actual race seat, not just a front-office title. Most F1 teams don't have that option. Aston Martin, through its factory Valkyrie Hypercar entry, does.

That also explains why the number under negotiation sits closer to Leclerc's than to a declining veteran's discount. Alonso isn't being priced as a driver whose current grid position justifies €40 million. He's being priced as a retention asset across two categories of racing and Aston Martin's wider commercial operation — the same logic that keeps aging stars on rosters in other sports well past their statistical peak, just with a literal second cockpit attached to the deal.

## Verdict

Alonso's delay only reads as indecision if you assume there are two outcomes: stay in F1 or retire. There are at least three, and he's negotiating all of them at once. My read: the WEC confirmation is the real news here, not the "not soon" — it's the leverage that lets him wait Aston Martin out on the Formula 1 salary number without risking the relationship at all. Expect a real decision closer to preseason testing, once the 2027 car is something Alonso has actually driven rather than something he's speculating about.
