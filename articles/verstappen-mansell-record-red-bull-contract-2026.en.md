## What Happened

Max Verstappen's win at Sepang on October 4 tied Michael Schumacher's record of 72 wins with a single team. That part made every headline. The part that didn't get the same treatment is the one that actually took longer to build: Verstappen's victory also broke the F1 record for the longest gap between wins at the same circuit, a mark Nigel Mansell had held for 34 years.

Verstappen first won at Sepang in 2017, his second career victory, for Red Bull. He won there again on Sunday -- 3,291 days later, as that year's Bahrain Grand Prix, relocated to Malaysia. The previous record, 2,325 days, belonged to Mansell, who won at South Africa's Kyalami circuit in 1985 and again in 1992. Verstappen's gap beat it by more than 900 days.

The win also extended a third streak: at least one victory in 11 consecutive seasons, 2016 through 2026. Only Schumacher and Hamilton have gone longer -- 15 seasons each.

## Why It Happened

The Mansell record exists because of a scheduling accident, not a training plan. Sepang hosted a round of the championship every year from 1999 to 2017, then dropped off the calendar entirely. It only came back in 2026 because the Bahrain Grand Prix needed a new home: the 2026 Iran war disrupted the Gulf region in the spring, and Formula 1, the FIA, Bahrain and Malaysia agreed to relocate the race to Sepang rather than cancel it. The nine-year gap between Verstappen's two Sepang wins isn't a stat he was chasing -- it's a byproduct of the only year since 2017 the calendar sent him back there.

The win itself was built on race pace that held through conditions designed to expose exactly the kind of new, undercooked power unit Red Bull-Ford Powertrains is running in its debut season: a start delayed roughly 100 minutes by rain and a software fault, two Safety Car periods, and a title contender's engine failing outright five laps from the end. [None of that touched Verstappen's car](/bahrain-gp-malaysia-2026-race-report-verstappen-72nd-win). He led every lap once the race actually started.

## Economic Impact

Red Bull signed Verstappen to [a new contract](/max-verstappen-net-worth-2026) in August 2026 -- reportedly worth close to €92 million a year in base salary from 2027, near €460 million in total value, running through 2030. At the moment that deal was signed, Verstappen had not won a single race that season. The bet Red Bull made wasn't on current form. It was on the proposition that a driver who had already won in 11 of the previous 11 seasons would keep winning for the length of a five-year contract, even through a season that started without a single victory.

Sunday is the first result that tests that bet, and it tests it in a specific way. To match Schumacher's and Hamilton's record of 15 consecutive winning seasons, Verstappen needs a win in every season from now through 2030 -- which is exactly the span his new contract covers. Red Bull isn't just paying for a driver who might win races again. The length of the deal and the length of the record chase line up almost to the year, which means the contract's full value only gets justified if Verstappen's 2026 win rate recovers and holds for precisely as long as Red Bull is paying for it.

There's a second number in this for Red Bull specifically. Through round 16, Verstappen sits sixth in the championship with 188 points and this one win -- a long way from the title fight Mercedes' Antonelli (320 points, eight wins) is currently running away with. A contract priced on multi-season reputation rather than this season's points table is a bet that F1's driver market, not the standings, sets a champion's price -- and Sunday's win is the first evidence since August that the reputation side of that bet still holds.

## The Framework

F1 driver contracts are priced on career trajectory, not trailing-twelve-month form, in a way most professional sports aren't. A striker who goes five months without a goal gets benched or sold; Verstappen went roughly five months into 2026 without a win and signed the richest contract of his career in the middle of that drought. The sport's economics tolerate -- even expect -- exactly this kind of lag between form and pay, because the pool of drivers capable of winning a championship is small enough that reputation, not recent results, is what actually gets priced into a multi-year deal.

That only works as a business model if the drought is temporary. Red Bull's 2026 is itself evidence of how that bet can go wrong in the short term: a new in-house power unit, a season that opened without a win for its lead driver, and a championship that, mathematically, Verstappen already can't win this year. The Mansell record and the Schumacher tie don't change any of that. What they do is supply the first data point since the contract was signed that the driver Red Bull is paying for still performs like the one they signed.

## Verdict

The headline record -- tying Schumacher -- was always going to get the attention, because it's the comparison to the sport's most famous name. The Mansell record is the more interesting one, because it's not really about Sepang at all. It's a nine-year gap that exists only because the calendar sent Verstappen back to a track he'd half-forgotten, and he won there again anyway. Red Bull didn't sign a €92-million-a-year contract for a driver who wins when the calendar cooperates. They signed it for a driver who's now shown, under a relocated race, a software fault, and a car still proving itself, that he still does the one thing the contract is actually paying for.
