## What happened

Williams team principal James Vowles has spent the last two weeks making a numerical case that Formula 1's cost cap has failed the midfield. His two headline figures: in the last 130 Grands Prix, only one has been won by a team outside the big four of Mercedes, Red Bull, McLaren and Ferrari -- Esteban Ocon's 2021 Hungarian Grand Prix for Alpine -- and teams outside that group used to reach the podium "10% of the time," and now reach it "5% of the time."

He also put a price on the gap. Williams, he told Motorsport.com, needs about $200 million of facilities to catch up with the top teams. Because facility spending is depreciated over "five to 10 years" inside the [$215 million cost cap](/glossary/cost-cap/technical/), that would take around $20 million a year out of Williams' budget for a decade. He says Williams pays "about 25%, 30% more than the top team for parts," which then arrive "about four weeks later." He wants the rules changed before the next regulation cycle, which he expects "for 2030 or 2031."

We checked both of his statistics against PaddockIntel's own results database, which holds every World Championship race since 1950. The first is exactly right. The second is right on its own terms, and still points at the wrong cause.

## Why it happened

Start with the win drought. Our data confirms it to the race: the last 130 Grands Prix run from the 2021 Emilia Romagna Grand Prix to the 2026 Singapore Grand Prix, and the only winner outside the big four in that span is Ocon in Hungary. Red Bull won 68 of them, Mercedes 26, McLaren 23 and Ferrari 12.

The podium figure needs a closer look. Across 2010 to 2020, teams outside the big four took 65 of 645 podium places -- 10.1%, which is Vowles' 10%. Across 2021 to 2026 they took 22 of 393, or 5.6%. But that 2010-2020 average blends two very different decades:

| Period | Races | Outsider podiums | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2010-2013 | 77 | 34 of 231 | 14.7% |
| 2014-2015 | 38 | 16 of 114 | 14.0% |
| 2016-2019 | 83 | 7 of 249 | 2.8% |
| 2020 | 17 | 8 of 51 | 15.7% |
| 2021-2025 (cost cap) | 114 | 21 of 342 | 6.1% |
| 2026, after 17 rounds | 17 | 1 of 51 | 2.0% |

Teams outside the big four, in this table and throughout, means every constructor other than Mercedes, Red Bull, McLaren and Ferrari. Podiums are Grand Prix podiums; Sprint results are excluded.

The collapse came in 2016, five seasons before the cap: across 2016 to 2019, the midfield managed seven podiums in 83 races. Over the first five cost-cap seasons, outsiders reached the podium more than twice as often as they did in those four years. The 2020 season, shortened to 17 races, sits on its own as the outlier between them.

Points tell a less flattering story for the cap, and here Vowles has the stronger case. The big four took an average of 78.9% of all points scored in 2016-2020 and 83.4% in 2021-2025. In 2026, after 17 rounds, the figure is 87.3%. Autosport, running its own version of this check, reached the same direction on points: 79% before the cap, 83% under it.

## Economic impact

The number that matters for Williams is not the podium share. It is the depreciation line. Twenty million dollars a year is 9.3% of the $215 million cap, and Vowles' own framing is that it would be spent every year for ten years just to reach facilities his rivals already own. A team spending that share of its capped budget on catching up has that much less for the car in each of those seasons. Add his 25-30% parts premium and the arithmetic compounds: a smaller effective budget, buying fewer parts per dollar, receiving them later.

That is also why his rivals' answers matter. Under the old rules, capital expenditure had its own separate allowance -- $36 million over five years, later moved to a sliding scale that gave the bottom teams about $20 million extra. From 2026 it sits inside the main cap. McLaren CEO Zak Brown told Autosport that combining capital and operating spending was "a step forward." Racing Bulls' Alan Permane said "if James wants to go and spend $100 million on something, he can do that." Toto Wolff said he has "super sympathy with a team like Williams" but doesn't "like the concept of adjustment based on some positions."

Permane's point is technically true and economically incomplete. Williams can spend $100 million; under straight-line depreciation it then carries that spend as reduced cap space for years, while a team that built its wind tunnel and simulator before 2021 carries nothing. The cap limits annual spending. It does not price in what each team already owned when the limit started.

The vote is the real constraint. Autosport reports that Vowles wants a change for the 2027 regulations, with 2028 as a fallback that, by his account, would need only four votes. The teams most likely to vote no are the ones whose pre-cap infrastructure is the advantage being discussed.

## The framework

The table hides a second pattern: midfield podiums arrive in bursts, one team at a time. Lotus took 24 podiums in 2012 and 2013. Aston Martin took 8 in 2023, which on their own are more than a third of the cap era's 21. The Enstone team -- Renault, then Lotus, then Alpine -- accounts for 40 of the 87 podiums won by teams outside the big four since 2010.

That matters for the policy argument. A podium share moves when one midfield team gets a car right for a season or two, which is exactly the kind of jump that needs facilities, tooling and headcount in place first. It is a capital question more than an annual-spend question. Vowles is describing the right bottleneck; the 10%-to-5% comparison just isn't the clearest way to show it, because it averages the best years of the 2010s against the worst stretch of the 2020s.

It is also why this connects to our [earlier analysis of the "cost cap trap"](/williams-cost-cap-trap-vowles-2026/): the cap froze the budget each team could spend, not the capital each team had already spent. And it is why Renault's chief executive said this week that [Alpine only pays back if it runs in the top three or four](/feed/renault-alpine-f1-losing-money-provost-top-four-2026/) -- the most successful midfield team of the period is still asking the same question Williams is.

## Verdict

The data points to a split verdict. Vowles' win statistic is exact, and the points concentration under the cap is real: 83.4% for the big four across 2021-2025, 87.3% so far in 2026. His podium statistic is accurate as quoted and misleading as evidence. The midfield's podium collapse happened in 2016-2019, under the old rules; the cost cap didn't cause it and hasn't reversed it.

My read: that makes his actual proposal stronger, not weaker. If the cap had driven the decline, tightening it would be the fix. Because the decline predates it, the lever that matters is the one he's pointing at -- how the rules treat capital that teams didn't get to spend before 2021. The 2026 season, with one outsider podium in 51, is the best argument he has. It just isn't the one he's been quoting.
