## What Happened

On lap 36 of the 2026 Azerbaijan Grand Prix, at the race's second Safety Car restart, Franco Colapinto locked up under braking into Turn 1. He hit his own Alpine teammate, Pierre Gasly, who was in turn pushed into Lando Norris's McLaren. All three cars retired on the spot.

Colapinto's own team radio left little ambiguity about what happened: "Sorry to all the team. Sorry to Pierre. Very bad, I completely locked up... I was locking up and completely out of control. So not a lot I could do." Stewards agreed it was his fault, issuing the standard 10-second time penalty for causing a collision. Because his car did not finish the race, that penalty cannot actually be served — stewards indicated it may be converted into a grid penalty for his next start, though that conversion has not been confirmed as applied.

George Russell went on to win the race by roughly two tenths of a second over Max Verstappen, in a Grand Prix stewards and press alike are already calling one of the most chaotic of the 2026 season.

## Why It Happened

Restarts compress a Grand Prix into a single high-risk braking event: a pack of cars that has been running to Safety Car delta times suddenly accelerates back to racing speed, arriving at the first braking zone closer together and faster than at any other point in the race. Turn 1 at Baku sits at the end of a long approach, which only sharpens how much speed has to be shed in a short space. Colapinto's own account — "completely locked up... completely out of control" — describes exactly that failure mode, without pointing to a mechanical cause. Nothing in reporting so far attributes the lockup to a car problem rather than a driving error at a genuinely difficult moment of the race.

## Economic Impact

The headline damage looks symmetric: three cars destroyed, three retirements, zero points for any of the three drivers involved. The real cost is not symmetric at all.

**McLaren's math:** Norris's points total was 186 before Baku and 186 after — the retirement did not subtract points he already had. What it cost him is different: a chance to add to that total while a rival gained. Russell's win took championship-leading Mercedes to 538 points, and McLaren's own static 306 now sits 232 points behind that total, up from a smaller gap before the race. In the constructors' championship, McLaren remains P3, unchanged in rank but further back in the number that actually decides prize money at the end of the season.

**Alpine's math:** Alpine's total also held flat, at 68 points, and the team remains P6 in the constructors' championship — 15 points behind Racing Bulls in P5. Losing both cars in the same incident is a bad weekend by any measure, but it happened in a part of the standings where the gap between finishing positions is worth far less, in both points and prize money, than the tier McLaren is fighting to hold. The same crash, the same zero points scored, landed on two teams with very different amounts actually at stake in that outcome.

**The unquantified line:** rebuilding three written-off chassis — two Alpines and a McLaren — is a real cost against each team's cost cap allocation for the season. Neither team has published a repair figure, and none should be assumed; it is a genuine cost, just not one this article can put a number on.

## The Framework

Formula 1's prize money is distributed by constructors' championship position, with real dollar gaps between adjacent places — a fact PaddockIntel has documented before in its breakdown of the 2025 payout structure. That means a team's response to a setback like this one depends entirely on where it sits in that table. For a team defending a mid-pack position against a rival separated by a handful of points, a scoreless weekend is a genuine threat to real money. For a team several rungs up the table chasing a rival that just extended its lead, a scoreless weekend is a missed chance to close a gap — arguably the more expensive outcome, because that gap does not reset next race, it compounds.

That is the asymmetry this crash exposes: identical inputs — one driver error, three wrecked cars, zero points across the board — produced two very different outcomes depending only on which team's championship position was on the line.

## Verdict

Colapinto pays the most visible price: a penalty he could not serve, a car he wrote off, and a radio message that offered no excuses. Alpine absorbs a bad Sunday it can recover from, in a part of the standings where the stakes are comparatively small. McLaren is the team that actually loses something durable — not points it had, but ground in a gap that just grew wider through no fault of its own driver. The crash that will be remembered as "the Colapinto incident" cost the team that wasn't driving it more than the team that was.
