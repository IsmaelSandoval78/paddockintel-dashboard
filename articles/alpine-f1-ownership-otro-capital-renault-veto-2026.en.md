## What Happened

On September 8, 2026, a three-year lock-up on Otro Capital's 24% stake in the Alpine F1 team expired. The restriction had been in place since September 7, 2023 — the day after Otro paid $200 million for the stake. With the lock-up gone, Otro is now free to sell, and it has made clear for months that it wants to.

It's already tried once. Renault, Alpine's majority owner, intervened to end talks between Otro and a group led by Mercedes' Toto Wolff in May 2026, after Otro's reported $720 million asking price was seen as too high by the Mercedes side. Christian Horner has since been named as an alternative bidder, reportedly seeking an actual shareholding rather than a passive stake.

In August, Alpine co-owner Flavio Briatore said an offer for Otro's 24% implied a $3.2 billion valuation for the whole team — up from the $200 million Otro paid for the same slice three years earlier.

## Why It Happened

The headline event is the lock-up expiring. The more consequential fact is what Renault can still do about it. Article 39 of Alpine's articles of association lets Renault veto a proposed buyer it classifies as "restricted" — a definition that reaches further than it sounds: entities in specific insolvency circumstances, competitors or businesses competing with Renault, parties connected to sanctioned or restricted jurisdictions, an automotive OEM's affiliate, or the operator of another F1 team. Renault gets up to three such vetoes before the restriction runs out.

That's not nothing, but it's not a wall either. A blocked bid can be revised and resubmitted under different terms or through a different vehicle, which is why market participants don't treat the veto as a serious obstacle to a sale eventually happening — only as a filter on who it happens with.

Renault's public posture backs that reading. At the British Grand Prix in July, Renault CEO François Provost dismissed Otro's relevance to the team outright: "Otro has no right, and no added value, to help us to operate." That's not the language of a company racing to close a deal. It's the language of a majority owner content to let a minority shareholder's exit run on Renault's clock, not Otro's.

## Economic Impact

Briatore's $3.2 billion figure is doing more work in headlines than in the actual negotiation. It's one shareholder's characterization of what one unconsummated offer implied — not a price anyone has paid, and not independently confirmed by a completed transaction. Otro's own math is more concrete: $200 million in 2023 for 24%, a $720 million ask in 2026 for the same slice. That's the number an actual counterparty — Mercedes — was presented with and walked away from as too high, not a valuation Alpine has to accept applies to the whole company.

The gap between those two figures — a stated $3.2 billion team valuation and a $720 million ask for less than a quarter of it that a sophisticated buyer already rejected as overvalued — is the real story. A minority stake in a business with no board control and no operational say, in a majority owner's own words, doesn't automatically price at a straight-line fraction of a headline valuation. Whoever buys Otro's 24% is buying a position Renault has publicly said adds nothing to how the team is run.

That's also the leverage Renault is exercising by not rushing. Every month Otro's stake stays unsold without a buyer at its asking price is a month that erodes the credibility of the $3.2 billion number, and strengthens Renault's position to let the next offer — or the one after — come in lower. Article 39's veto matters less as a legal weapon here than as the thing that lets Renault set the pace of that erosion instead of Otro. It's the same underlying pattern PaddockIntel found in [Audi's appeal over a single championship point](/audi-tsunoda-appeal-italian-gp-2026): the headline dispute is rarely the real one, and the real one is almost always priced in money neither side says out loud at first.

## The Framework

This is the shape a growing number of F1 ownership disputes will take as team valuations keep climbing faster than any actual liquidity event confirms them. A stake purchased for $200 million against a real, disclosed price is a data point. A valuation claimed by an interested party about an offer that didn't close is a marketing figure, not a market price — no different in kind from a startup's post-money valuation after a round that never actually priced.

Article 39-style veto rights exist across F1's ownership structures precisely because a grid slot is worth more today than at almost any point in the sport's history, and every incumbent owner wants control over who gets to hold a piece of that appreciation — not just whether someone can pay for it. The same appreciation shows up on the driver side of the ledger: see PaddockIntel's breakdown of [Fernando Alonso's 2027 salary negotiation](/fernando-alonso-f1-future-2027-salary-negotiation), where a driver's market price is similarly a function of leverage and timing, not a fixed number anyone simply looks up.

## Verdict

Otro Capital will likely sell its Alpine stake eventually — Renault's veto is a filter, not a lock. My read: the number that ends up on the sale agreement will land far closer to the $720 million Mercedes already rejected than to Briatore's $3.2 billion valuation claim, because Renault has every incentive to wait out that gap and no incentive to help close it.
