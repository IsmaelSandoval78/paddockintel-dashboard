## What happened

Formula 1 recorded $922 million in payments to its teams in the first nine months of 2026, against $968 million in the same period of 2025, according to the quarterly report Liberty Media filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission on November 5. In the third quarter alone, with seven races against six a year earlier, team payments rose to $422 million from $341 million. F1's revenue rose 28% to $1,115 million, and its adjusted OIBDA, the operating profit measure Liberty reports, rose 44% to $338 million.

Over nine months, revenue was flat at $2,496 million. Fifteen races were held against 17: the Saudi Arabian Grand Prix could not take place in April, and the calendar now runs to 23 rounds after Malaysia hosted the Bahrain Grand Prix in October. "Every race this season has sold out, with more than half setting attendance records," Formula 1 chief executive Stefano Domenicali said in the results release.

## Why it happened

F1 recognises most of its media rights, sponsorship and team payments across the season in proportion to the races held, so its quarterly figures move with the calendar. The third quarter carried 7/23rds of the season against 6/24ths a year earlier; the first nine months carried 15/23rds against 17/24ths. That explains both the jump in the quarter and the flat nine months. The 2025 figures also included one-off media rights revenue from the release of the F1 movie, the filing notes.

Underneath the calendar, the business kept growing. By PaddockIntel's calculation from the filing, primary revenue, which covers race promotion fees, media rights and sponsorship, came to $137 million per race held in the first nine months of 2026, against $123 million a year earlier. That is an increase of about 11%, with the movie revenue still in the base. Liberty attributes it to contractual fee increases across all streams and to new sponsors.

## Economic impact

The teams' share is set by contract. Under the 2026 Concorde Agreement, the prize fund is calculated as a fixed percentage of Formula 1's Prize Fund Adjusted EBIT, an operating profit adjusted for largely non-cash items, Liberty's 2025 annual report says. Ferrari receives a share for its heritage; the rest is paid on results in prior constructors' championships, "a significant majority" of it on the previous year's position.

On the same pro rata basis as the filing, the first nine months point to about $1.41 billion for 2026, close to the $1.40 billion recorded for 2025. The estimate is ours and is not a forecast. In 2025, the same calculation from nine months gave $1.37 billion and the final figure came in $33 million higher, because the variable elements of the prize fund are set on full-year revenue and costs.

What changes is how many teams share it. With Cadillac on the grid, 11 teams split the fund instead of ten, so on our estimate the average payment falls from $140 million in 2025 to about $129 million, unless the pot grows faster in the fourth quarter. The existing teams were compensated for that dilution through the one-off fee Cadillac paid to join, as we explained in [our analysis of F1's entry fee](/byd-geely-f1-entry-fee-anti-dilution-2026).

The order matters more than its timing suggests. Because most of the fund follows the previous year's table, the places settled in Qatar and Abu Dhabi decide most of each team's 2027 payment: Red Bull's nine-point lead over McLaren for third and Haas's three points over Audi for seventh, in PaddockIntel's database. F1 does not publish the amount per position, which is why "a hell of a lot of money from P4 to P3", as Lando Norris told RaceFans, remains a description rather than a figure. [Our Las Vegas analysis](/las-vegas-gp-2026-race-analysis-352-overtakes-what-changed) sets out the odds for each fight.

## The framework

Over three years, F1's revenue has grown faster than its payments to teams. Revenue rose from $3.22 billion in 2023 to $3.87 billion in 2025, about 20%; team payments rose from $1.22 billion to $1.40 billion, about 15%. The share of revenue paid to teams fell from 37.7% to 37.1% and then 36.1%, and stood at 36.9% in the first nine months of 2026.

That follows from the design of the deal rather than a squeeze: the fund tracks adjusted profit, not revenue, and F1's own costs, from hospitality and freight to FIA fees, rose as well, the filings show. It also explains why the teams watch F1's costs as closely as its income. And because the cost cap limits most team spending (see [how the cap reshaped the midfield](/f1-cost-cap-midfield-podiums-vowles-data-2026)), a larger prize payment mostly improves a team's accounts rather than its car.

## Verdict

The filings say the teams' pot is holding at about $1.4 billion while one more team takes a share, and that F1's business per race is still growing at about 11%. The money being decided in the last two races is mostly next year's.

My read: 2026 is a strong year for F1 and a flat one for the average team. Unless the variable elements surprise again in the fourth quarter, the average payment per team is set to fall for the first time in the three years the filings cover, from about $122 million in 2023 and $127 million in 2024 to $140 million in 2025, then about $129 million. That makes the final places in Qatar and Abu Dhabi worth more than their points suggest.
