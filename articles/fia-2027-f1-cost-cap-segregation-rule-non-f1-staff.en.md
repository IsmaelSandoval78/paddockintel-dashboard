## What Happened

The FIA has written a wall into Formula 1's 2027 rulebook. Article F6 of the 2027 Operational Regulations, published on October 19, 2026 and approved by the World Motor Sport Council the same day, is titled "Physical and IT segregation between F1 activities and non-F1 activities". The Council's statement described it as new requirements "in regard to the Financial Regulations", the rules behind the cost cap.

The principle is in the first clause. Staff who work on F1 may not enter the area used by staff who work only on the group's other businesses, or open the files those staff produce, "at any point in time". The same applies the other way round. The FIA can check through physical inspections of team facilities and "IT forensic audit procedures and controls".

The article covers every entity in an F1 team's legal group, except those that do only F1 work, or only non-F1 work, and share no facilities or restricted IT systems with the other side. RacingNews365 names the kind of operation in its sights: Red Bull Advanced Technologies, Aston Martin Performance Technologies and Mercedes' Applied Science division.

## Why It Happened

The cost cap limits what a team can spend on its F1 car, at US$215 million for a 2026 season of 24 races or fewer. It works only if the line between F1 spending and everything else holds. According to Autosport, until now a team could split an engineer's time between F1 and another programme and count only the F1 percentage against the cap. That leaves the other share of the same person's know-how on the books of a sports-car, Formula E, sailing or product-development project.

Article F6 stops relying on how time is booked and starts controlling where people sit and what they can open. Non-F1 staff work from a "Restricted Non-F1 Area"; F1 staff work from a "Restricted F1 Area"; each must be either a separate building or a dedicated part of one, with an electronic access system. Messaging apps, external drives, service providers, staff moves and intermediaries are all named as routes a team must not use to get around the rule.

## Economic Impact

The cost lies in what a group can no longer share for free. Expensive tools stay shared, but in shifts: wind tunnels, driver-in-the-loop simulators, dynamometers, test benches and rigs, data centres and meeting rooms may be used by both sides "although never at the same time". When the F1 team is in the room, the space counts as F1 territory. Where an IT system cannot be split virtually, the team must run two copies of it, one for each side.

People are where the rule bites hardest. To move someone from a non-F1 project into the F1 team, a group must give them a new contract, withdraw all their IT access, and keep them away from both F1 and non-F1 work and from all restricted systems for at least three months. After the move they must stay on the F1 side for at least 12 months. A move out of the F1 team works the same way without the three-month gap, followed by 12 months on non-F1 work only. In both directions, the person's pay for the whole reporting period of the move counts as F1 cost, subject to the exceptions in the Financial Regulations.

Knowledge can still flow one way. F1 staff may pass a file to the non-F1 side if it is "strictly necessary", after a written request approved and recorded by designated senior F1 staff, with any explanation held by video call, recorded and transcribed. The rule is explicit that nothing produced by non-F1 staff may go back the other way.

Autosport's analysis expects the change to raise staff numbers and costs, as non-F1 programmes hire from outside instead of borrowing from the F1 team. Under the cap, three months of a salary with no work attached and a full year's pay booked as F1 cost both make a transfer expensive.

## The Framework

The cap has been tested before: the FIA found that Red Bull exceeded the 2021 limit by £1.86 million, or 1.6%, and Red Bull accepted a US$7 million fine and a 10% cut in aerodynamic testing. The rule also lands on a debate about how far the cap reaches. Christian Horner said this month that teams spend "$500 or $600m" against a cap of just over $200 million; Mercedes' 2025 accounts show about $619 million in operating costs. We set out where that gap goes in [F1's $215M cost cap vs Mercedes' $619M in costs](/f1-cost-cap-real-team-spending-horner-mercedes-accounts/). Most of it is spending the cap excludes on purpose, such as driver pay and marketing, which the new article leaves alone: it does not widen the cap, it polices its edges.

Some work stays on the F1 side of the line by definition. Supplying power units, supporting customer teams and taking part in FIA projects are not treated as non-F1 activity, and the CEO, team principal, chief information officer, board members and staff who do only functions such as marketing, HR, finance or legal work may work from either area. For what the cap does and does not cover, see [what is included and excluded from the F1 cost cap](/f1-cost-cap-what-is-included-and-excluded/).

## Verdict

The data says the FIA has moved from trusting a team's timesheets to controlling its floor plan, its logins and its staff moves, with three years of records for inspectors to read. My take: the teams that pay are those that sit inside larger engineering groups and have used them as a flexible pool of people and tools. A team whose only business is F1 has nothing to separate. From 2027, a group can still own a wind tunnel, a simulator and a sports-car programme, but sharing them with the F1 team now costs time and paperwork, and every move books a full reporting period of that person's pay as F1 cost, with three idle months on top for anyone joining the F1 team. That is the cost of making the $215 million line mean the same thing for every team.
