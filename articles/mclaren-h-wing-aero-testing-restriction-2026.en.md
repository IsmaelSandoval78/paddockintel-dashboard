## What Happened

McLaren will not run its rotating H-wing at the Madrid Grand Prix. The part — a rear wing that rotates 180 degrees between a low-drag "Straight" mode and a high-downforce "Corner" mode — made its competitive debut at Monza. For Madrid's inaugural race at the new MADRING circuit, McLaren reverts to its older Zandvoort-specification wing instead, and plans to bring the H-wing back at Baku.

The team's stated reasoning is straightforward: MADRING is a high-downforce layout, and the H-wing's main advantage — reduced drag on long straights — isn't worth much there. Baku's long main straight is a better fit.

That explanation is true. It's also incomplete. McLaren isn't just choosing the right tool for the track. It's choosing where to spend a resource it has less of than any other team on the grid.

## Why It Happened

Since 2021, F1 has capped how much time each team can spend in the wind tunnel and running CFD (computational fluid dynamics) simulations — the Aerodynamic Testing Restriction, or ATR. The allocation isn't fixed. It's a sliding scale tied directly to where a team finished in the previous season's constructors' championship, recalculated twice a year.

The mechanics for the first half of 2026: a baseline of 100% equals 320 wind tunnel runs and 2,000 CFD simulations, assigned to the mid-table reference position. Every position better than that costs 5% of the allocation; every position worse adds 5%. McLaren won the 2025 constructors' title — its 10th, and a second straight, sealed at Singapore with six rounds to spare. That result sets its 2026 H1 band at 70%: 224 wind tunnel runs, 1,400 CFD simulations. Alpine finished 2025 last and sits at 115%: 368 runs, 2,300 simulations. Cadillac, entering the grid fresh, was assigned the same band as the team it replaces at the bottom.

The rule exists to stop a dominant team from out-developing the field twice over — first with the biggest budget, then with the most testing time to spend it. It works as designed. It also means McLaren enters its title defense with the smallest testing budget in the sport, at the exact moment it needs development pace to hold off Mercedes and Ferrari.

## Economic Impact

The gap between McLaren and Alpine isn't cosmetic: 144 fewer wind tunnel runs, 900 fewer CFD simulations, over the same six months. Every run and every simulation is a chance to validate whether a new part behaves the way the model predicted before it's built and bolted to the car. Fewer runs means less room to validate a concept everywhere before committing to it — so a team with McLaren's allocation has to choose, track by track, where a given upgrade is worth confirming and running versus where the previous, already-proven package stays on the car.

That's the real logic behind parking the H-wing at Madrid. It isn't just that the part helps less there. It's that every additional configuration a team runs at a race is another data point it has to have modeled and validated in advance, against a testing budget that's already the tightest in the field. Running a track-specific wing everywhere would demand a validation workload McLaren's 2026 allocation doesn't comfortably support. Running it only where the payoff is largest — Baku's long straights, not MADRING's tight infield — rations that scarce validation capacity toward the tracks where it matters most.

This is happening while McLaren is fighting from third in the 2026 constructors' standings — 285 points, 61 behind Ferrari and 183 behind a dominant Mercedes, with ten rounds still to run. A title defense usually calls for more development, not less. McLaren has to find it with 224 wind tunnel runs instead of the 320 a mid-table team gets, because it won the thing it's now trying to defend.

Mercedes isn't much better off. Finishing 2025 second gives it a 75% allocation — 240 runs, 1,500 simulations — tighter than every team except McLaren, even as it leads the 2026 standings by 122 points over Ferrari. The top of the field is development-starved by rule, not by budget. ## The Framework

ATR is F1's second cost-control lever, and it works on a different axis than the $215 million spending cap. The cap limits how much money a team can spend. ATR limits how much a team can *iterate*, independent of budget — a team with unlimited cash still can't buy its way to more wind tunnel runs than the sliding scale allows. It's the aerodynamic equivalent of a reverse draft order: finish on top, and the system hands your competitors more chances to test their way back into contention than it gives you to extend the lead.

The pattern extends past this one wing. Any team finishing 2025 in the top half enters 2026 running a leaner, more track-specific upgrade cadence than a team finishing in the bottom half — not because the front-runners are more disciplined, but because they're structurally forced to be. ## Verdict

McLaren parking the H-wing at Madrid isn't a one-off track call — it's a preview of how the rest of 2026 will look for every team ATR punishes for winning. Expect more selective upgrade rollouts from McLaren and Mercedes through the back half of the season, and a wider gap in how freely Ferrari, sitting one place lower on the sliding scale, can afford to experiment. The team best positioned to out-develop the field in 2026 isn't the one with the biggest budget. It's the one with the most room left on the sliding scale to use it.
