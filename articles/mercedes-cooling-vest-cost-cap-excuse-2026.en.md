## What Happened

George Russell and Kimi Antonelli raced without cooling vests at a heat-hazard Grand Prix held at Sepang, Malaysia, with cockpit temperatures reported above 40°C and the track surface far hotter. Russell told The Times the decision was "a direct consequence" of Formula 1's season-long cost cap, and that Mercedes "haven't put the resource into making it robust enough" to run the vest reliably. He asked the FIA to either add extra money to every team's cap specifically for this, or have an outside supplier build a vest for the whole grid. Max Verstappen, who didn't use one either, dismissed the complaint: "I don't need it... you shouldn't exaggerate." Ferrari and McLaren drivers, including Carlos Sainz, raced with vests.

This wasn't Russell's first brush with F1 heat. He needed medical attention for dehydration and heat exhaustion after the 2023 Qatar Grand Prix — the incident that pushed the FIA toward heat-hazard protocols in the first place. His complaint carries a real safety history behind it, not just discomfort.

## Why It Happened

Russell's quote names the cost cap as the constraint. The regulations say something more specific. Per Autosport's reporting on the 2026 financial rules, the FIA didn't leave cooling-vest development to compete against every other item on a team's capped budget — it carved out two allowances specifically for it: $50,000 for a system proven to work a full race distance, plus a further $75,000 for improving comfort and effectiveness, stacking to as much as $125,000 of extra room outside a team's normal spending fight. That detail doesn't appear in Russell's public framing, and neither Crash.net's nor PlanetF1's coverage of his comments mentions it.

Mercedes was mid-crisis on car development at the time — the team was running comparison tests between its old and new W17 packages at the following round, unsure which specification was actually faster. Choosing to leave cooling-vest development short, with a mid-season upgrade push competing for the same engineers and budget, is a defensible call. But it's a call about where Mercedes wanted its people and money to go, not evidence that the cap made a working vest impossible to build.

## Economic Impact

Scale the number. A $125,000 allowance against a $215 million full-season team cost cap is six-hundredths of one percent of what Mercedes was allowed to spend in 2026. That's not a budget squeeze — it's a rounding error relative to a single aerodynamic upgrade package, let alone a season's development program. If Mercedes genuinely couldn't spare that fraction of its cap to finish a safety-adjacent piece of equipment it already had a specific allowance to build, the story is about internal prioritization, not a regulation blocking the work.

That distinction matters because "the cost cap" is F1's most legible shorthand for "we didn't have the money," and it's increasingly doing rhetorical work the actual numbers don't support. (For what actually sits inside and outside the $215 million limit, see [PaddockIntel's cost cap breakdown](/f1-cost-cap-what-is-included-and-excluded).) Williams' James Vowles has a real, documented version of this complaint — [PaddockIntel's own reporting on his "cost cap trap"](/williams-cost-cap-trap-vowles-2026) showed the sport's top four teams now take 83% of all points, up from the 70s before the cap existed, because a flat spending ceiling locks in whichever team already had its infrastructure built. That's a structural argument about the cap's overall design, backed by season-long competitive data. Russell's vest complaint is a different kind of claim — about one specific, small, already-exempted line item — wearing the same "cost cap" label. Lumping them together flattens a real structural critique and a team's own spending choice into the same sentence.

## The Framework

F1's cost cap is a single, well-publicized number, which makes it a convenient explanation for almost any team decision a driver or principal wants to justify publicly — whether or not the specific item in question is actually capped the way the explanation implies. That's a predictable consequence of regulating with one headline figure: it becomes the default answer to "why didn't you," accurate or not, because it's the number everyone already knows and nobody outside the team can easily check against the actual exemption schedule.

Verstappen's blunt dismissal ("you shouldn't exaggerate") reads as a rivalry jab, but it's also, incidentally, the paddock's own informal fact-check — a competitor who raced the same heat without a vest and isn't buying the cap as the reason one wasn't ready. The FIA's allowance structure is the more useful check: a regulator that carves out a specific, stacking budget line for a piece of safety equipment has already decided that equipment shouldn't be a casualty of the cap. Whether a team uses that room is on the team.

## Verdict

Russell's underlying safety concern is real, sourced to his own medical history, and worth taking seriously on its own terms. His explanation for why Mercedes didn't have a working vest in Malaysia isn't. The FIA set aside up to $125,000 in cap room for exactly this equipment — a fraction of one percent of what Mercedes was allowed to spend this season — and the team chose to put its engineers elsewhere. That's a legitimate call about priorities under a hard budget. It's not a cost cap stopping anyone from building a cooling vest.
