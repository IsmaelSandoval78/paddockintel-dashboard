Kimi Antonelli's 2026 Mercedes salary is not public. Reported base figures range from $2 million to £2.6 million, with performance bonuses estimated at up to $7.5 million to $12 million on top. Mercedes has confirmed none of them. Every figure below is an estimate published by the outlet named, not a number from Mercedes or Antonelli.

QuiFinanza reports that his bonuses are tied to race wins and championship position, so a title this season would put him at the top of the bonus range; the exact terms have not been published.

## What Happened

Andrea Kimi Antonelli enters the back half of the 2026 season leading the drivers' championship by 81 points, with eight wins from fourteen rounds. After [the Spanish Grand Prix](/madrid-gp-2026-race-hamilton-dnf-mclaren-ferrari-gap/) at round 14, both Mercedes teammate George Russell (211 points, second) and Lewis Hamilton (191 points, third) have publicly conceded the title race is over. Antonelli is 20 years old and in his second F1 season.

His salary tells a different story than his results. Spotrac, citing RacingNews365, puts his 2026 Mercedes base at $2 million — a figure repeated across most English-language salary trackers. Gazzetta dello Sport, reporting in July 2026, put it instead at £2.6 million (roughly $3.3-3.5M at 2026 exchange rates). Mercedes has confirmed neither number; the team's only public statement on his contract, in October 2025, covered the 2026 driver line-up and said nothing about pay.

Whichever base figure is closer to reality, it sits well below what his results would command on the open market. That gap — not the exact dollar figure — is the story worth tracking.

## Why It Happened

Antonelli's pay structure is a standard rookie deal: a conservative base, with the real money loaded into performance bonuses that only pay out if results justify them. He signed with Mercedes on a one-year deal for 2025, then a one-year extension for 2026, confirmed alongside Russell's own extension in October 2025. F1 Salaries estimates his bonus potential at up to $12 million for 2026 — on top of the disputed base, that puts his ceiling near $13-14 million if he hits every trigger.

Multiple outlets report Mercedes has secured him through 2029, though the team itself has never confirmed contract length beyond 2026. If accurate, that detail matters more than the salary figure: it means Mercedes controls when the renegotiation conversation happens, not Antonelli. He has no equivalent of an exit clause forcing the team's hand early.

That's a deliberate structure, and it's the same one Red Bull used on a young Max Verstappen a decade ago — sign the talent cheap before the market knows what it's worth, then renegotiate once it does.

## Economic Impact

The clearest way to price what Antonelli is worth right now isn't his own contract — it's what Red Bull just paid to avoid losing a driver he's beating by 147 points. Verstappen's Red Bull deal contained an exit clause that activated when he entered the summer break outside the championship's top two, 110 points behind Antonelli. Red Bull responded in August with [a new contract through 2030](/max-verstappen-mercedes-2026/), reported by F1 business journalist Marc Limacher at roughly €92 million a year in base salary — up from Verstappen's previous $70 million — with performance bonuses potentially pushing total pay past €115 million. Red Bull and Verstappen haven't confirmed the figures, but formula1.com has confirmed the extension itself.

Read that against Antonelli's reported $2 million: Red Bull just paid an incremental $37M+ a year, on top of an already elite salary, to retain a driver currently 147 points and six wins behind the guy earning a fraction of it. That's not a comment on Verstappen's talent — it's what an exit clause costs a team when a rival driver's results make the seat look suddenly replaceable. Antonelli's contract has no equivalent clause. Mercedes doesn't have to match anyone's market rate until it chooses to.

Russell's situation sits in between. Spotrac lists his base at $34 million; RacingNews365, Motorsport.com and PlanetF1 report a guaranteed $40 million plus up to $8 million in bonuses, with a one-plus-one structure that auto-renews for 2027 if he hits performance targets. Russell negotiated that deal off a genuine track record — 319 points and two wins in 2025. He's now 81 points behind a teammate on a rookie base a fraction of his own, which is exactly the scenario his contract's auto-renewal clause wasn't built to price.

None of this means Antonelli is being cheated — a $2 million-plus-bonuses rookie deal is standard practice, and Mercedes bore the actual risk of signing an unproven 18-year-old. But the Verstappen number puts a real figure on what that risk is now worth once it pays off: the gap between what a team pays to keep a proven, aging asset and what it still owes a younger one already outperforming him is where the actual money in this story lives.

## The Framework

F1 teams routinely underprice young drivers relative to veterans, then pay a premium later to correct the gap — but who captures that gap depends entirely on contract mechanics, not performance. A driver with an exit clause, like Verstappen, forces the team to renegotiate the moment a rival's results make him a flight risk. A driver without one, like Antonelli through 2029 by most reporting, has no mechanism to force that conversation regardless of how the standings look.

That's the actual lesson of this season's two Mercedes-adjacent contract stories: Red Bull didn't pay Verstappen more because he got better in 2026 — by the numbers, he got worse, sitting sixth with zero wins before the new deal. It paid more because its own contract gave Verstappen leverage at the exact moment a teenager two garages over made the alternative look cheap. Mercedes, by contrast, gets to watch Antonelli win eight races without a single clause requiring it to respond.

## Verdict

The $2 million figure was never the real number in this story — it was always a placeholder, priced before Antonelli had started a race. The real number is what Red Bull just agreed to pay to keep a driver he's already beating by 147 points: something north of €92 million a year, activated only because a contract clause forced the issue. Mercedes faces no equivalent deadline. My read: every additional win Antonelli takes between now and 2029 raises the size of the check Mercedes will eventually have to write — it just doesn't have to write it yet, and that's the whole advantage of the contract it signed.
