## What Happened

Texas paid $40,977,419 toward the 2025 United States Grand Prix at the Circuit of the Americas, according to the Events Trust Funds award spreadsheet the Texas Governor's Office updated on 30 September 2026. The money came from the state's Major Events Reimbursement Program and was received in April 2026. It is the largest amount the spreadsheet lists for any US Grand Prix since it starts in 2015, ahead of $40.28 million for 2022.

The same spreadsheet shows the crowd moving the other way. The attendance certified to the state for 2025 was 113,157, down from 136,841 in 2021: four straight declines and 17% in total. The organizer's own estimate for 2025 had been 136,060. For this week's race, the 2026 application estimates 110,320, 19% below last year's estimate.

PaddockIntel summed the spreadsheet's US Grand Prix rows: about **$296.3 million across nine editions** (2015-2017, 2019 and 2021-2025). There is no row for 2018, and there was no race in 2020.

## Why It Happened

The payment isn't a price per ticket. Under the program's guidelines, an event's trust fund is sized on the estimated increase in tax receipts the event generates in the local market, from five taxes only: sales and use, hotel occupancy, vehicle rental, mixed beverage and wholesale alcohol. The local partner pays in first and the state matches it at up to 6.25 to 1. Attendance is a check, not the formula: if the certified crowd comes in 25% or more below the estimate, the state may cut the fund in proportion. The 2025 shortfall against the estimate was 16.8%, inside that line.

So what moves the number is who comes and how much they spend in Texas. In 2015, 50.6% of certified attendees came from outside the state. In 2025 it was 72.8%, the highest share in the spreadsheet. A visitor from out of state pays hotel tax and rental-car tax that an Austin resident doesn't, and that spending is exactly what the formula counts.

## Economic Impact

Divide the payout by the certified crowd and the trend is clear. In 2021 Texas paid about **$260 per certified attendee**; in 2025 it was about **$362**, up 39%. Those per-head figures are PaddockIntel's arithmetic, not a rate the state sets, but they show what a smaller, more out-of-state, higher-spending crowd does to a tax-based formula: the state's bill goes up while the headcount goes down.

How much of the $40.98 million is state money depends on the local share actually paid in. If the local partner paid the full match, the 6.25-to-1 rule puts about $35.3 million on the state and about $5.7 million on the local side. That split is a calculation from the published ratio; the spreadsheet only reports the total.

The size is clearer next to the other world championship at the same track. COTA's MotoGP round, the Red Bull Grand Prix of the Americas, received $8.39 million for 2025 from the same program, with 41,336 certified attendees. Formula 1 drew 2.7 times the crowd and 4.9 times the money.

Formula 1 counts its crowd differently. It reports that the race has drawn more than 430,000 fans in recent years, a weekend count, and in October 2025 it extended the race at COTA through 2034. The state's figure is the attendance certified against the trust fund, the number it checks its payment against.

## The Framework

Every Grand Prix host pays for the race one way or another; the difference is where the bill shows up. In Singapore, the government co-funds 60% of a S$135-140 million annual cost, as we laid out in [Singapore's hosting fee and ticket economics](/singapore-gp-2026-economic-impact/). In Qatar and Abu Dhabi the fee itself is the negotiation, which is why [their roughly $100 million standoff](/f1-2026-qatar-abu-dhabi-hosting-fee-standoff/) is holding up the end of this calendar. Texas does it through the tax code: the state doesn't set a fee, it reimburses the organizer's eligible costs up to an estimate of the tax the race brings in.

That design has one weakness on view in this spreadsheet: it rewards spending per visitor, not visitors. It also depends on paperwork. For 2018 the Governor's Office said the required human trafficking prevention plan had not been filed 30 days before the race, and that reimbursement could not be issued; the spreadsheet lists nothing for that year, while the years either side are around $27 million.

## Verdict

The data says the US Grand Prix is getting more valuable to Texas per fan and less crowded by the state's own count. My read: with a contract through 2034 and a formula that pays for out-of-state spending, Texas is now paying for a smaller, richer race, and the 2026 estimate of 110,320 suggests the organizer expects that to continue. This week's race may settle the title, as [Antonelli's Austin math](/antonelli-title-austin-2026-qatar-abu-dhabi-math/) shows; the number to watch when the 2026 row fills in is not the crowd but the payout per head.
