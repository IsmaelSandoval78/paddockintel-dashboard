## What Happened

Formula 1 team principals now expect a final call on whether the Qatar Grand Prix (round 22, November 29) and the Abu Dhabi Grand Prix (round 23, December 6) go ahead as scheduled to come by mid-October — weeks later than F1 CEO Stefano Domenicali's original mid-September target, which passed without a decision. Haas team principal Ayao Komatsu said he believes "Stefano is targeting to tell us in mid-October so we all know what the situation is." Audi's Mattia Binotto called mid-October "the last call for such a decision."

The uncertainty traces back to the same source that already cost F1 two races this year: the ongoing conflict between the United States and Iran, which the UK Foreign Office describes as leaving the region "unpredictable" with "a risk of more attacks and unforeseen escalation." Bahrain and Saudi Arabia's Grands Prix, originally set for April 12 and 19, were both cancelled outright. Bahrain chose not to simply lose its slot — [its round was reinstated at Malaysia's Sepang International Circuit on October 4 instead](/colapinto-crash-cost-mclaren-championship-2026), funded by Bahrain itself as what F1 has described as a gesture of support for the sport, the first Formula 1 race at Sepang since 2017.

Qatar and Abu Dhabi are a different problem: unlike Bahrain, there's no alternative venue lined up that either side has committed to. Imola, in Italy, has been placed on standby as the contingency to close the season if the Middle East rounds can't go ahead.

## Why It Happened

The five-month gap between "conflict starts" and "decision still not made" isn't indecision — it's the contract. Under F1's commercial agreements, cancelling a race carries a real cost, and the contracts assign that cost based on who cancels, not on why. If a promoter pulls out of its own race, it still owes Formula One Management the hosting fee. If FOM cancels the race instead, FOM forfeits that payment. Neither Qatar nor Abu Dhabi has moved to cancel, and neither has F1 — each side is better off waiting for the other to blink, or for the situation to resolve on its own.

That standoff is visible in how Abu Dhabi's promoters are behaving: continuing ticket sales, promoting entertainment at Yas Marina Circuit, and maintaining a normal official presence at other F1 events, all consistent with a race that still expects to happen rather than one bracing to be cancelled.

## Economic Impact

Neither Qatar's nor Abu Dhabi's hosting fee is officially published, but industry reporting puts them at roughly $55 million a year for Qatar and $42 million for Abu Dhabi — a combined figure close to $100 million annually. Those numbers are informed estimates, not disclosed contract terms, and F1 hosting agreements vary enough between promoters that a precise combined figure isn't independently verifiable.

The number that matters more than the annual fee is the exposure if both races are lost: reporting puts total financial exposure at $190-200 million once hospitality, sponsorship activation, and lost EBITDA around the two events are included, not just the sanctioning fees themselves. That is the figure FOM is weighing against the cost of racing in a region its own drivers are publicly split on.

The Imola contingency has its own, smaller cost line: F1 has said it will cover the work needed to prepare Imola as a finale venue — grandstands and supporting infrastructure — even if that preparation ultimately goes unused because the Middle East rounds proceed. No figure for that contingency spend has been disclosed either, but it is a real, currently-accruing cost sitting on top of whatever the Qatar/Abu Dhabi decision ends up costing.

None of this is about championship points or race-day competition — Verstappen and Hamilton's public disagreement is about whether F1 should go at all, not about who wins if it does. [PaddockIntel's breakdown of how F1 prize money actually splits](/melbourne-2026-f1-prize-money-constructors-championship) covers the sporting-side payout structure; the Qatar/Abu Dhabi numbers here sit entirely on the commercial, promoter-to-FOM side of the business, a separate ledger from anything a driver or constructor earns on track.

## The Framework

State-backed Grands Prix — Qatar, Abu Dhabi, Bahrain, and the now-cancelled Saudi Arabian round — don't behave like conventional promoter contracts, because the buyer isn't trying to turn a profit on ticket sales alone. A national government treats an F1 slot as a soft-power and tourism asset, which is exactly why Bahrain spent its own money to relocate rather than simply pocket a cancellation and walk away: losing the slot costs more in visibility than keeping it costs in relocation logistics. It's also why Qatar and Abu Dhabi haven't cancelled themselves even with a live safety argument in the paddock — for a state promoter, being seen to cancel under pressure can carry its own cost, separate from the hosting fee itself.

That asymmetry is the actual reason this keeps getting punted forward instead of resolved: a conventional commercial promoter facing a $50 million annual fee against genuine safety uncertainty might cut losses early. A state promoter weighing reputational stakes alongside the money has a very different calculation, and F1's own commercial interest — not forfeiting up to $100 million in fees it would rather collect — points the same direction. Nobody with the power to cancel first has an incentive to be the one who does it.

## Verdict

The public argument between Verstappen ("when there is a war nearby... I think we shouldn't go") and Hamilton ("I do hope that we get to go there") reads like a straightforward safety debate, and for the drivers it is one. But it isn't what's actually setting the timeline. The decision is being paced by a roughly $100-million-a-year contract standoff over who forfeits money by moving first, with Imola quietly on retainer as the answer nobody has to admit they're planning for yet. Mid-October is the date that resolves it — not because the security picture will necessarily be clearer by then, but because that's the point at which logistics stop allowing anyone to keep waiting.
