## What Happened

Christian Horner wants Formula 1 to put more of a team's spending inside the cost cap. "What I see now is that you've got a budget cap of just over $200m but teams are spending $500 or $600m," the former Red Bull team principal told Autosport, as reported by Motorsport.com. "More needs to be included within the cap." His image for what the cap does: "It's like a balloon with not enough air in it, if you squeeze it one end, the air is going to go somewhere else."

The same week, Mercedes' 2025 accounts gave his number something to be checked against. Mercedes-Benz Grand Prix Limited reported revenue of £633.4 million for the year to December 31, 2025, operating profit of £166.7 million and profit after tax of £125.9 million, according to its filing at Companies House. More than £415 million of the revenue came from sponsorship and licensing. The team paid £125 million in dividends to its shareholders, up from £100 million in 2024, and its directors wrote that it was confirmed in full compliance with the 2024 Financial Regulations after the FIA audit.

## Why It Happened

The cost cap was never a cap on total spending. The 2026 limit is US$215 million for a season of 24 races or fewer, under the FIA's Financial Regulations, and it applies to defined "Relevant Costs": mostly the work of designing, building, developing and racing the car. A long list sits outside it. F1 driver pay is excluded, as is the pay of a team's three highest-paid eligible staff, marketing, heritage work, HR, finance and legal functions, and specified travel and catering. Power units are governed by a separate set of FIA financial regulations. We map the full boundary in [what the F1 cost cap covers and what it doesn't](/f1-cost-cap-what-is-included-and-excluded/).

That is the balloon Horner describes. The cap started at US$145 million in 2021, and from 2026 Formula 1 says annual depreciation now counts inside it as well. But a team's revenue is not capped, and the money a team earns beyond the limit still gets spent, on the lines the regulations leave open. Horner named one of them: "You'll now see driver retainers far healthier than they were pre-budget cap."

## Economic Impact

The arithmetic on Mercedes is simple. Revenue minus operating profit leaves about £466.7 million in operating costs for 2025. At about $1.33 to the pound, the rate RacingNews365 used to convert the same accounts, that is roughly **$619 million**, inside the range Horner gave and almost three times the 2026 cap. The 2026 cap is higher than the one Mercedes raced under in 2025, so the comparison flatters the cap, not the spending. The accounts cover the team company; power units fall under separate rules and are a separate question.

On top of those costs, Mercedes still made £166.7 million in operating profit and returned £125 million to its owners. The cap did not stop the richest team spending; it stopped the spending from turning into losses. We looked at what that does to the team's value in [Mercedes F1 is worth $6B and still leading the standings](/mercedes-f1-valuation-wolff-business-strategy-2026/).

At the other end of the grid, the cap figure is a ceiling some teams cannot reach from their own revenue. Haas's UK company reported a record £147.4 million in revenue for 2025, per RacingNews365, about $195 million at the same rate, below the $215 million cap before a single excluded cost is paid. A cap set for everyone binds hardest on the teams that can afford to spend up to it.

Horner's own former team shows how large the excluded lines can be. Red Bull Racing's and Red Bull Technology's filings record a combined £100.48 million in "compensation for loss of office" to their highest-paid director, RacingNews365 reported; the filings do not name Horner, who was the highest-paid director before his exit. How such a payment is treated against the cap is for the FIA's auditors; what is public is that the regulations exclude the pay of a team's three highest-paid staff. For scale: the overspend that got Red Bull sanctioned for 2021 was £1.86 million, 1.6% over the cap, and cost it a $7 million fine and a 10% cut in aerodynamic testing. We totalled what firing team principals has cost the grid in [the price of sacking an F1 team principal](/f1-team-principal-firing-cost-2026/).

## The Framework

The cap equalises one slice of spending, the slice that makes the car faster, and leaves revenue and everything around it to the market. Mercedes' revenue in 2025 was 4.3 times Haas's. Both were held to the same cap on the car. That is why the cap made teams profitable without making them equal: Alpine made money in 2025 finishing last, Williams lost money finishing fifth, as the same set of filings showed.

It is also why the argument about the cap is now an argument about which lines to move inside it. Williams team principal James Vowles says the frontrunners benefit most, because teams that built their facilities before 2021 can put the whole cap into the car; we tested his data in [Vowles' cost cap stat, checked](/f1-cost-cap-midfield-podiums-vowles-data-2026/). Horner wants driver retainers and other exempt spending pulled in. Each proposal moves money between columns. None of them touches revenue, which is what sets how much air is in the balloon.

## Verdict

Horner's number holds up. On Mercedes' own accounts the biggest teams do spend around $600 million, and a $215 million cap covers roughly a third of it. My read: the cap has done what it was built to do, which is stop teams spending themselves out of existence; Mercedes paid its owners £125 million last year. It was never built to equalise total spending, and the filings show it doesn't. Moving more lines inside the cap would narrow the gap only if revenue stopped finding the lines that remain outside, and nothing in the 2025 filings suggests it would.
