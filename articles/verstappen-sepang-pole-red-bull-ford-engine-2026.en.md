## What happened

Max Verstappen took pole position for the Bahrain Grand Prix on October 3, 2026 — a race relocated to Sepang, Malaysia after April's scheduled date in Bahrain was cancelled over the continuing conflict in the Middle East, with the Gulf state still funding the event and keeping its name. His lap of 1:35.130 put him 0.298 seconds clear of Ferrari's Lewis Hamilton, with Mercedes' championship leader Kimi Antonelli third and Ferrari's Charles Leclerc fourth. It was Red Bull's first pole of the season, and Verstappen didn't hide what it meant: "We've been trying for many Grands Prix to get more, sometimes a bit closer to the others... to finally get it from where we started the season, it's pretty incredible." Asked what changed, he pointed not at the engine but at the car around it: Red Bull had "understood our car a lot better" and put "a lot of good stuff" on it in recent races.

## Why it happened

That answer matters, because the FIA had already settled the engine question months earlier. Under the sport's ADUO system — a cost-cap relief mechanism, not "a kind of balance of performance," per FIA single-seater director Nikolas Tombazis — manufacturers measured behind a performance benchmark earn extra development tokens to catch up. Red Bull-Ford's DM01 power unit was rated the benchmark itself: zero tokens. Mercedes, roughly 2% behind, got one token for 2026 and one for 2027; Ferrari, Audi and Honda, measured 4% or more behind, got two each year. By the FIA's own torque, engine-speed and MGU-K measurements, Red Bull built the best power unit on the 2026 grid — before it had scored a single point with it. What held the car back instead was the chassis: the RB22 started the season roughly 12kg overweight, with energy-recovery and aerodynamic problems that cost over a second a lap in Australia and China, and that Verstappen and teammate Isack Hadjar both called barely manageable by Hungary. The Miami upgrade halved the weight deficit; a seven-part package at Austria and a final round at Baku chased down the rest. Sepang is where that work and the engine's measured advantage finally lined up on the same lap.

## Economic impact

This is what Christian Horner meant when he called building Red Bull Powertrains "the biggest single investment that Red Bull have ever made in motorsport" — a project forced on the team when Honda walked away after 2021, now run in partnership with Ford, which holds no equity stake but contributes financially and technically to the 350kW MGU-K, turbocharger and testing rigs in exchange for naming rights. No official total has been disclosed; company accounts show roughly $90 million in fixed-asset purchases for the powertrains program in 2021 alone, before a single lap under the new rules was run, and spending has continued every year since. The ADUO system is explicitly built to help the teams furthest behind, funded by the same cost cap that applies equally to everyone — which means the manufacturers getting development tokens right now are, by definition, the ones whose own engine programs haven't yet matched what Red Bull built from a standing start. Red Bull gets nothing from that mechanism, because the only thing it needed to prove — that its own power unit was competitive — the FIA already confirmed for free.

## The panorama

It's the same infrastructure logic running in both directions. [James Vowles says the cost cap traps smaller teams](/williams-cost-cap-trap-vowles-2026) by freezing in place whatever advantage a top team already had before the cap existed; Red Bull's engine program is the counterexample on the supply side — proof that a team willing to make the single largest bet in its history, with real risk of falling short, can use the same capped era to build an advantage from scratch rather than just defend one it already had. [Red Bull's broader technical reset](/f1-news-red-bull-craig-skinner-leaves-chief-designer) — Christian Horner's exit, Laurent Mekies now in charge, longtime chief designer Craig Skinner departing mid-transition — was always going to be judged against whether the new engine era it was built around actually worked. Not everyone in the paddock is convinced that era is worth defending: Sky Sports' Karun Chandhok has argued publicly that F1 would be better off on simpler, non-hybrid engines, pointing out that manufacturers are sinking real capital into a format he thinks the FIA got wrong. Red Bull's pole doesn't settle that argument. It just proves that, within the rules as written, the money it spent bought exactly what it was supposed to.

## Verdict

Sepang doesn't prove Red Bull's engine bet "worked" in the only sense that will eventually matter — wins and championships, not lap times and technical audits. But it closes a gap that had existed all season between two separate verdicts on the same power unit: the FIA's, reached off dynos and torque curves months ago, and the stopwatch's, reached on a Saturday in Malaysia. For most of 2026, those two verdicts disagreed, and the car's own weight and aerodynamics were the reason why. They agree now. What happens Sunday, and for the rest of the season, is still an open question. Whether Red Bull built the right engine for 2026 isn't, anymore.
