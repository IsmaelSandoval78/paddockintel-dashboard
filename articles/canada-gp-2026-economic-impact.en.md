Formula 1 returns to Montreal on May 22–24, 2026 for the Canadian Grand Prix — one of the sport's most commercially potent race weekends and the only F1 event in North America that consistently draws over 300,000 fans to a single venue.

Contracted through at least 2035, the event has become a cornerstone of Montreal's economy, exporting its financial footprint to a global audience for one high-octane weekend.

## The Economic Engine

Tourisme Montréal estimates the annual economic impact of the event at approximately **$162 million CAD**, with over 85% of spending originating from non-Quebec visitors. Provincial and municipal governments collected over **$9 million** in tax revenue associated with the event.

The contract extension projects nearly **$1 billion in tourist spending** over four years, adjusted for inflation — a figure that makes the hosting fee paid by Quebec to Liberty Media look like a modest investment by any commercial standard.

Hotel rates tell the clearest story: average nightly rates in central Montreal peak at **US$658 during race weekend**, a 167% increase over baseline. The seasonal short-term rental ban projected for 2026 is expected to compress accommodation supply further, pushing those rates even higher.

## What Tickets Cost in 2026

Ticket prices for the 2026 Canadian Grand Prix increased **20–26% year-on-year** in Canadian dollars — one of the highest increases on the 2026 calendar.

A full weekend in central Montreal averages **$860 CAD ($630 USD)** per person, excluding flights:

- **General Admission** — Access to grassy hillside areas. The most affordable entry point and, for experienced fans, the best atmosphere.
- **Grandstands 15, 21, 24** — Best sightlines for racing action at the Épingle hairpin, where position changes happen.
- **F1 Experiences** — Hospitality packages starting at $999 USD, including pit lane walks and paddock access.

The 20%+ price increase reflects a market dynamic Formula 1 has exploited across its entire calendar since the Netflix era: demand has outpaced supply, and promoters have priced accordingly.

## The 2026 Calendar Twist

The 2026 race moved from its traditional June window to **May 22–24**, placing it in direct conflict with the Indianapolis 500. This creates a rare clash of icons — forcing broadcasters and sponsors to split resources between F1's North American flagship and IndyCar's crown jewel on the same weekend.

For Liberty Media, the calendar shift is a calculated bet: Montreal's 300,000-fan attendance figure and $162M economic footprint gives them leverage with the Quebec government, justifying the date change despite the broadcast complications.

For teams, the earlier date compresses the development calendar between Bahrain and the European swing, increasing the premium on reliability over outright performance at the season's midpoint.

## The Prize Money Structure

There is no per-race payout for winning in Montreal — Formula 1's $1.266 billion annual prize pool is distributed at season's end, weighted by final Constructors' Championship position.

Every point scored at the Circuit Gilles Villeneuve feeds directly into that year-end calculation. With **Kimi Antonelli leading the 2026 championship** entering Montreal and new regulations generating genuine uncertainty across the grid, the Wall of Champions remains one of the most commercially loaded barriers on the calendar.

A single race result at Montreal can shift a team's final prize payout by **$8–12M** depending on how tightly the midfield is compressed. For teams operating near the cost cap ceiling, that margin is the difference between a development upgrade and a budget freeze.
