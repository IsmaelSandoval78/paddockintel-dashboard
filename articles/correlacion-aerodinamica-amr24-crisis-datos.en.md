## What Happened

Through round 16 of the 2026 season, Aston Martin has scored 7 points. That's 10th of 11 constructors, ahead of only Cadillac, and every one of those points belongs to Fernando Alonso — Lance Stroll has none. [PaddockIntel already covered what that specific split means for Stroll's seat](/aston-martin-alonso-stroll-2027-ownership-2026). This piece is about why the car itself has been this far off the pace in the first place.

The season started worse than the current total suggests. Aston Martin took a single point from the first 11 grands prix, with Alonso and Stroll both reporting a car that was difficult to drive on top of a Honda power unit that was down on power and short on reliability. A large upgrade package at the Hungarian Grand Prix changed the trend — Alonso reached Q2 for the first time all season, and described the car as more predictable, with more grip to work with. Progress, but from a very low base, on a car that was supposed to be the first full product of Adrian Newey's design group and a new, purpose-built development campus.

## Why It Happened

The 2026 car was always going to carry risk: a switch from customer Mercedes engines to a new works Honda deal, built around the biggest Formula 1 engine rules reset since 2014. What made it worse was a tool problem that predates this season. Aston Martin brought its first-ever in-house wind tunnel online at its Silverstone campus starting in 2025, ending its long-standing arrangement using Mercedes' tunnel. Ahead of the 2026 season opener, Sky Sports' Martin Brundle reported that the correlation between that new tunnel, the team's CFD models, and actual lap times on track looked "miles out" — based on only 400 laps completed across pre-season testing in Barcelona and Bahrain, itself a sign of how little track data the team had banked.

Correlation problems are not cosmetic. If a wind tunnel and CFD model don't agree with what the real car does on a real track, a team can't trust which upgrades will actually work before building and fitting them. Team principal Andy Cowell had already pointed to the same new-tunnel-versus-old-data mismatch to explain why the 2025 car underperformed — meaning the tool Aston Martin bet its whole 2026 design philosophy on arrived without a fully trusted baseline, in the same year the team also switched engine suppliers.

## Economic Impact

The wind tunnel at the center of this isn't a minor line item. The full AMR Technology Campus — new factory, first in-house wind tunnel, new simulator — cost Aston Martin roughly £200 million, funded by Lawrence Stroll's ownership group specifically to remove the team's dependence on a rival's facility and put Adrian Newey's design process on infrastructure it fully controlled. [PaddockIntel covered the scale of that broader bet already](/adrian-newey-aston-martin-2026-investment-crisis). A tool that expensive is supposed to compress development time, not add a correlation problem on top of a new-engine integration the team was already absorbing.

The return on that investment is legible in the standings, not just the car. Ten rounds into the current form guide, 7 points is a number sponsors and the team's own investors can see as easily as any fan can — and unlike a single bad result, it's not explained away by one weekend's conditions. A correlation problem that takes months to resolve means development hours spent re-establishing trust in the tool itself, hours that produce no lap-time gain and show up nowhere in the points column, before a single hour goes toward the car getting genuinely faster.

## The Framework

F1 teams have run into new-facility correlation problems before — it's a known risk of bringing a wind tunnel in-house rather than renting time on an already-calibrated one. What makes Aston Martin's version worth watching as a pattern is the stacking: a new facility, a new engine partner, and a new lead designer's first full design cycle, all landing in the same regulatory reset year. Any one of those three is a real source of risk on its own. Together, they remove the option of isolating which variable is actually responsible when the car underperforms — which is a large part of why it took until the Hungarian Grand Prix, roughly two-thirds of the way through the season, for the team to say with any confidence that the tools were finally pointing the same direction as the stopwatch.

## Verdict

Aston Martin didn't lose its 2026 season to bad luck. It lost the first half of it to a trust problem between a very expensive new tool and the car it was supposed to be designing. The Hungaroring upgrade bump is real evidence that trust is coming back — Alonso's own description of a more predictable car is the kind of qualitative signal that tends to show up before the points do. My read: the £200 million campus was always going to be judged on 2027 and 2028, not 2026, and the correlation problem that defined this season's first half is the specific, nameable reason that judgment got delayed by a year it didn't have to.
