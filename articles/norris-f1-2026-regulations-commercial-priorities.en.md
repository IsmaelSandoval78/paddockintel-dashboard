## What Happened

Lando Norris, F1's reigning world champion, said this week that it's "a shame" Formula 1 is so heavily influenced by commercial decisions — and named the specific one he means. The 2026 power unit's roughly 50-50 split between combustion and electrical power, he said, exists "because more teams wanted to come in it and because you make more money," not because it produces better racing.

It's a sharper version of a complaint Norris has been building toward all season. Back in February, before the new cars had turned a competitive lap, he waved off driver grumbling about the incoming rules: "We get paid a stupid amount of money to drive, so you can't really complain in the end of the day." Five months and a season's worth of energy-management-restricted racing later, Norris is the one naming the specific commercial trade-off he thinks the sport made — and saying drivers had no seat at the table when it was made.

## Why It Happened

The 2026 power unit regulations were locked in years before a single 2026 car turned a wheel, and they were written with a specific outcome in mind: getting a new manufacturer onto the grid. Increasing the electrical contribution of the power unit toward near-parity with the combustion engine was central to that pitch — a technical direction that plays directly to what a manufacturer already investing heavily in electrification wants to demonstrate on a global stage.

Audi took that pitch. The brand entered Formula 1 for 2026 as a full works team, built on its takeover of the Sauber operation. Norris isn't disputing the logic — he's stating it plainly: "in order for Audi and other teams to come in we had to change these things."

The on-track consequence is the part drivers are unhappy about. Heavy energy-management demands have restricted flat-out speed at power-sensitive circuits, producing what multiple drivers this season have called "yo-yoing" performance through a lap — pace that depends less on a driver's raw input and more on how well a car's software manages a limited electrical budget. Crash.net reported drivers warning the format is "waiting for it to go horribly wrong."

## Economic Impact

This is where the story stops being a driver complaint and becomes a business story. Formula 1 has never pretended the Concorde Agreement is written purely for sporting reasons — the anti-dilution fee that a new team like Cadillac pays to join, or the entry terms that governed Audi's own path onto the grid, are explicitly commercial instruments dressed as regulation. What Norris is describing is the same logic applied one level deeper: not just who's allowed to enter, but how the cars themselves are engineered, shaped in part by what makes the entry proposition attractive to the manufacturer being courted.

Audi's return on that regulatory environment is, so far, modest — ninth in the 2026 constructors' championship with 10 points. But the cost of the trade-off is spread across the whole grid, not billed only to the new entrant. Every team operating under the cost cap is now allocating a larger share of a fixed development budget to energy-deployment software rather than pure aerodynamic or mechanical performance — the same category of spending behind the Mercedes calibration fault that hit both Russell's and Antonelli's cars at Spa this season. A regulation written to make F1 commercially attractive to one new manufacturer reshaped the engineering priorities, and the cost structure, of every team on the grid.

## The Framework

Formula 1's business logic is usually visible in the places PaddockIntel already tracks — hosting fees, sponsorship deals, entry payments. Norris's comments make explicit something usually left implicit: that the technical regulations themselves, the rules that determine what the cars can do on track, are shaped by the same manufacturer-acquisition calculus as the financial ones. It's not a new phenomenon in motorsport — technical standards bodies in other industries have shaped specifications to court a valuable new entrant before — but it's rare for a reigning world champion to say so mid-season, in public, without much hedging.

## Verdict

Norris isn't wrong, and he's not the first driver to think it. What makes this notable is who's saying it and how directly: the reigning champion, naming the specific regulation, the specific manufacturer it was written for, and the specific trade-off — money for racing quality — that resulted. Whether that trade was worth it depends on whether you're pricing Formula 1 as a fan or as a business. Norris's answer, in his own words, is that F1 priced it as the business.
