## What Happened

George Russell topped the first-ever Formula 1 practice session at Madrid's new Madring circuit, clocking 1:34.077 on Friday — 0.286 seconds ahead of Mercedes teammate Kimi Antonelli, who leads the championship by 66 points with 10 rounds remaining. Charles Leclerc was third for Ferrari, Lewis Hamilton fourth on the harder medium compound, and Max Verstappen fifth. Lando Norris, the reigning champion, was sixth.

At the other end of the timesheet, Aston Martin had a quieter session: Fernando Alonso finished 15th, teammate Lance Stroll 19th — in the same 60 minutes where Alonso told reporters, hours earlier, that his decision on 2027 depends on next year's rules, not on how his current car performs.

## Why It Happened

Russell called the new circuit the "risky" one on the calendar; Verstappen went further, calling it a potential "car killer" — real assessments of a street layout with concrete walls close to the track and corners no driver had taken in an F1 car before Friday. On a genuinely new surface with unknown grip evolution, a clean, incident-free session from the championship leaders is itself the story: Mercedes managed the unknown better than most of the grid did on lap one.

Aston Martin's session tells a plainer story. The team has spent most of 2026 outside the podium picture, and nothing about a new, unfamiliar circuit changes that baseline. Alonso's own public position — that his 2027 call depends on the rulebook, not on Aston Martin's current pace — gets harder to square with a session where his team was again closer to the back of the midfield than the front.

## Economic Impact

One practice session doesn't move a championship, but Antonelli's 66-point lead heading into round 14 is close to doing exactly that with ten rounds left, and the money attached to that math is real: F1's 2026 prize pool reportedly pays constructors' champions around $140 million against roughly $131 million for second place, a gap of about $9 million per position built into a formula that rewards the standings, not any single weekend. [Mercedes has separately been valued at $6 billion this year](/mercedes-f1-valuation-wolff-business-strategy-2026/) — a season that ends with Antonelli's points lead intact reinforces that number to sponsors and investors, independent of what any single Friday practice session shows.

Aston Martin's side of the ledger looks the opposite way. The team is [already committed to nine figures rebuilding around Adrian Newey](/adrian-newey-aston-martin-2026-investment-crisis/), and Alonso is [reportedly negotiating a raise from €30 million to €40 million](/fernando-alonso-f1-future-2027-salary-negotiation/) on the explicit logic that his decision hinges on next year's car, not this one. A 15th-place Friday doesn't settle that negotiation either way — but it's one more data point that the value Aston Martin is paying for isn't current lap time, because current lap time isn't there to sell.

## The Framework

Formula 1's new-market push — Madrid included, on the back of a reported €467 million regional economic case — depends on exactly the kind of session Mercedes just delivered: fast, clean, headline-friendly, with the championship protagonists at the front of the timesheet on a circuit nobody has raced before. An incident-heavy debut session would have been a worse story for the sport's broader case for new venues than a comfortable Mercedes one-two, regardless of how either result affects Sunday.

That's also why a single practice session gets over-read industry-wide, and why this one shouldn't be: fuel loads, tyre programs, and track evolution differ by team in ways a public timesheet doesn't show. Hamilton's fourth-fastest time came on the medium compound, a real step behind the soft-tyre runners ahead of him on pace-per-lap terms — a detail that matters more for Saturday's qualifying simulation than for judging Ferrari's actual competitive position on lap one.

## Verdict

Take the timesheet for what it is: one clean session for the two drivers already leading the championship, and one rough one for a driver renegotiating his contract on the promise that next year's car will be different from this year's. My read: neither fact changes anything by Sunday, but both reinforce the money question already sitting under each team — Mercedes defending a $6 billion valuation with a lead that's turning into cash, Aston Martin trying to justify a raise with a car that still isn't finishing practice sessions near the front.
